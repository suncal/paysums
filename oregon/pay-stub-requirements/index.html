Oregon pay stub requirements (2026)

Required by state law? Yes — and from 2026, an explanation of the stub at hiring

Oregon already required a detailed itemized statement. From 1 January 2026, Senate Bill 906 adds something no other state asks for: at the time of hire the employer must hand over a written explanation of what every line on that statement means, including a description of each payroll code — and review it every January.

What must be on an Oregon pay statement

ORS 652.610 requires a written statement, sufficiently itemized to show:

  • the date of the payment, and the dates of work covered by it
  • the employee's name
  • the employer's name, business registry or business identification number, address and telephone number
  • the rate or rates of pay
  • gross wages and net wages
  • the amount and purpose of each deduction
  • any allowances claimed as part of the minimum wage

Two of these are stricter than most states. Oregon wants the employer's business registry number and telephone number on the statement, not just a name and address. And it wants the purpose of each deduction, not merely the amount — a line reading “misc. $42.00” does not satisfy the statute.

New for 2026: the written explanation at hire

Senate Bill 906 was signed on 28 May 2025 and takes effect 1 January 2026. It requires every employer to give every new hire a written explanation of the earnings and deductions that appear on the itemized statement. As enacted, that explanation covers the regular pay period; every category of pay rate the employer uses — hourly, salary, shift differentials, piece rate, commission; every deduction and its purpose; benefit deductions and employer-provided benefits; any allowance claimed toward the minimum wage; and, unusually, the payroll codes used for pay rates and deductions together with a description or definition of each code.

That last item is the point of the law. Oregon decided the problem was not that stubs lacked information but that nobody could read them — the abbreviations in the deduction column mean nothing without a key. Employers must also review and update the information by 1 January of each year, so this is a recurring duty rather than a one-off document.

Two different penalties

These are easy to conflate and the summaries often do. The Commissioner of the Bureau of Labor and Industries may assess a civil penalty of up to $500 for violating the new written-explanation requirement. That sits alongside the separate, pre-existing penalty of up to $1,000 attached to unlawful payroll deductions under the wage statutes. They address different conduct and an employer can face both.

What an Oregon pay stub actually shows

Oregon has no sales tax and comparatively high income tax, so the stub is deduction-heavy. State income tax runs on four brackets — 4.75%, 6.75%, 8.75% and 9.9% — against a small standard deduction of $2,910 single and $5,820 married, with an exemption credit of $256 and $512. Oregon is one of the few states that lets you subtract federal income tax, capped at $8,250 and phased out at higher incomes, which is why Oregon withholding is less predictable than a flat-rate state's. On top of that sit two statewide payroll deductions: the statewide transit tax at 0.1% of wages, and Paid Leave Oregon, whose employee share is 0.6% of wages up to $184,500. Employees in the Portland area may also pay Metro Supportive Housing Services and Multnomah County Preschool for All taxes on income above $125,000 — separate lines again. Questions about Oregon pay stubs

What changes for Oregon employers on 1 January 2026?

Senate Bill 906 takes effect. Every new hire must receive a written explanation of the earnings and deductions shown on their itemized statement — including a description of each payroll code used — and the employer must review and update that information by 1 January each year. The itemized statement requirements under ORS 652.610 themselves do not change.

What is the penalty for breaking Oregon's new pay statement rule?

The BOLI Commissioner may assess a civil penalty of up to $500 for a violation of the written-explanation requirement. That is separate from the existing penalty of up to $1,000 for unlawful payroll deductions — different conduct, different penalty, and both can apply.

Does an Oregon pay stub have to say what a deduction is for?

Yes. ORS 652.610 requires the amount and the purpose of each deduction, so an unexplained line does not satisfy it. From 2026 the employer must additionally have given you, at hire, a key explaining the payroll codes those deductions appear under.

What are the transit tax and Paid Leave deductions on my Oregon stub?

The statewide transit tax is 0.1% of wages and funds public transport outside the existing transit districts. Paid Leave Oregon is the state's paid family and medical leave programme — the employee share is 0.6% of wages up to $184,500, with the employer paying the rest. Both are separate from state income tax.

Why does Oregon withhold less than my bracket suggests?

Because Oregon lets you subtract federal income tax from Oregon taxable income, up to $8,250 and phased out as income rises. It is one of a handful of states that still allows this, and it makes Oregon withholding harder to estimate by hand than a flat-rate state's.
