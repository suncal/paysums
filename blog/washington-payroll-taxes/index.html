Washington Payroll Taxes for Employers: No Income Tax, Still File

Published October 14, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Washington has no general personal income tax, so employers withhold no state income tax. You still handle unemployment insurance, Paid Family & Medical Leave, WA Cares, and workers’ compensation, plus federal payroll taxes. Below we cover who pays what, the rates and wage bases, filing deadlines, and a payroll setup checklist.

TL;DR:

  • For 2026, Paid Leave is 1.13% of wages up to $184,500; employers with 50 or more employees owe 28.57% of the premium.
  • WA Cares withholds 0.58% from employees on every dollar of wages, without a Social Security cap; employers do not match it and must report quarterly.
  • ESD sets annual UI rates using capped benefit history, a social tax capped at 1.22%, and an administration fund contribution; notices arrive in December.
  • Workers’ compensation premiums depend on hours worked, risk classification, and claims history, so track each employee’s hours under the correct classification.
  • Quarterly reports are due April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31; employers must file even when they had no payroll.

What Washington payroll taxes cover and what they don’t

Washington currently has no general individual income tax, so there is no state income tax line on a Washington paycheck. That does not mean payroll is simple. Federal withholding still applies, and the state runs several programs that each have their own rules.

Here is how obligations map to payroll:

  • Federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare: withheld from employees, with matching employer shares for Social Security and Medicare.
  • Unemployment insurance (UI): paid by the employer.
  • Paid Family & Medical Leave (Paid Leave): shared between employer and employee, with rules that depend on employer size.
  • WA Cares Fund: paid by the employee only.
  • Workers’ compensation: premiums based on hours worked, paid to the Department of Labor & Industries (L&I), with an employee share withheld.

One forward-looking point: a new 9.9% income tax begins January 1, 2028 for individuals and joint filers with adjusted gross income above $1 million. It does not change withholding for typical employees, but it is worth knowing if you pay high earners. For the federal side, our note on 2026 federal tax withholding changes is a useful companion.

How Washington unemployment insurance rates and wage bases work

UI is an employer-paid tax, and the Employment Security Department (ESD) sets your rate each year. Your rate is built from three parts: an experience rating based on your own history of benefit charges (capped), a flat social tax (capped at 1.22%), and a contribution to the Employment Administration Fund (EAF). Because the experience rating moves with your claims history, two employers in the same industry can pay very different rates.

New employers start at a default rate until they build enough history to be rated, and the federal minimum rate can influence what you owe. ESD publishes the taxable wage base history, including the 2026 figure, on its rates page. The wage base is the maximum wages per employee that are subject to UI tax in a year, so your payroll system must stop calculating UI once an employee reaches it.

You receive your rate notice in December, effective January 1. ESD also offers a rate calculator and an employer rate lookup, which are worth running before you finalize annual budgets.

Your UI checklist:

  • Reconcile quarterly taxable wages against payroll reports.
  • Keep records of benefit charges so you can dispute errors.
  • Run the ESD calculator when preparing payroll for a new year.

Pro Tip: Compare the December rate notice to the rate loaded in your payroll system every January. A stale rate is one of the easiest errors to miss.

Paid Family & Medical Leave premiums, shares, and reporting

For 2026, the Paid Leave premium is 1.13% of gross wages, up to the 2026 Social Security cap of $184,500. The Employment Security Department confirmed the increase and the split in its 2026 rate announcement.

Employees may pay up to 71.43% of the premium, and employers pay the remaining 28.57% when required.

The employer share applies to businesses with 50 or more employees. Smaller businesses are not required to pay the employer share, but they must still collect the employee share and report quarterly.

Here is what that means in practice:

  • You either withhold the employee share from wages or pay it yourself. You cannot skip it.
  • You cannot retroactively collect missed premiums from employees, so correct setup at the start of the year matters.
  • Quarterly reports and premium payments are required for every employer.
  • Withholding stops once an employee reaches the Social Security cap for that employer.

When configuring payroll, apply the cap per employee, per employer. If an employee changes jobs midyear, each employer tracks wages against the cap separately. Set the rate to apply to gross wages, and test with a high earner’s paycheck to confirm withholding stops at the right point. The rate is recalculated every October, so update your settings each year.

WA Cares Fund: who pays and how to report

WA Cares is a separate, employee-funded program. The premium is 0.58% of gross wages, and unlike Paid Leave it has no Social Security cap, so it applies to every dollar of wages. Employers do not match it.

  • Who pays: employees only. You withhold it, or you cover it yourself if you choose.
  • How it is reported: Paid Leave and WA Cares appear on a combined quarterly report, but the programs are funded separately and tracked separately.
  • When to file: every quarter, including quarters with no payroll.

The employer wage reporting and premiums toolkit walks through withholding, hours and wages reporting, and the no-payroll report. Keep it handy during setup.

Workers’ compensation basics for Washington payroll

Washington’s system is unusual. Premiums are based on hours worked rather than a percentage of payroll, and your specific rate depends on your industry risk classification and your claims history. That makes accurate hour tracking a payroll task, not just an HR one.

To get an account, apply for or update your state business license. The process also helps you find your L&I account manager.

  • Track hours precisely for every employee, by classification.
  • Keep job classifications accurate, since a misclassified worker can mean a misapplied rate.
  • Contact your L&I account manager with questions about rates or classifications.

Workers’ compensation is also one piece of an employer’s wider risk picture. For context on how liability coverage fits alongside it, this explainer on employers liability coverage is a helpful read.

Pro Tip: Export hours by classification each quarter and compare them to your L&I report before you submit.

Employer registration and account setup in Washington

Registration runs through the Department of Revenue (DOR). Registering for state payroll taxes is built into the Business License Application, and your Unified Business Identifier (UBI) number links your accounts. Once your license is updated, ESD and L&I are notified so they can set up your accounts.

Update your license about 90 days before your first pay date so account information arrives in time. Follow these steps:

  1. Apply for or update your business license through the DOR application.
  2. Confirm your UBI number is active.
  3. Watch for account numbers and instructions from ESD and L&I.
  4. Log in through SecureAccess Washington to reach agency accounts.
  5. Enter each account number in the matching field of your payroll system before the first run.

Quarterly filing, deadlines, and no-payroll reports

Washington employers file quarterly. Reports are due by the last day of the month following the quarter, which gives you these dates:

  • April 30 for the first quarter
  • July 31 for the second quarter
  • October 31 for the third quarter
  • January 31 for the fourth quarter

Reports list total wages and detailed employee information, and the rule specifies accepted formats and exceptions. See ESD’s guide on how to file your quarterly tax and wage reports.

If you had no payroll in a quarter, you must still file a “no payroll” report. Skipping it leads to penalties and interest. Your annual UI rate notice arrives in December, and the new rate takes effect January 1.

A step-by-step checklist for setting up Washington payroll

Use this sequence when you configure or audit a payroll system:

  1. Confirm your DOR, ESD, and L&I accounts are active and recorded.
  2. Set the Paid Leave rate at 1.13% and the WA Cares rate at 0.58%.
  3. Build UI taxable wage base logic so tax stops at the annual limit.
  4. Configure hours tracking by classification for workers’ compensation.
  5. Set up no-payroll reporting and a reminder for each deadline.
  6. Schedule quarterly reconciliations of payroll output against agency reports.

The Social Security cap applies to Paid Leave per employee, per employer, and does not apply to WA Cares. Keep payroll registers, rate notices, and agency invoices together so you can reconcile each invoice against what you reported.

Pro Tip: Run a test paycheck for one low earner and one earner near the cap each January. If both come out right, the rest usually will too. Our Washington paycheck calculator is a quick way to check the expected result.

Common payroll mistakes and how to avoid them

Most Washington compliance problems come from a short list of errors:

  • Retroactive collection: do not try to recover missed Paid Leave or WA Cares premiums from employees later. The responsibility stays with you.
  • Missing no-payroll reports: file them on time to avoid delinquent status and penalties.
  • Wrong classifications or hours: inaccurate L&I classifications or hour counts lead to misapplied premiums.
  • Stale rates: failing to update rates after the December notice or the October Paid Leave recalculation.

If you catch an error, correct your payroll settings first, then contact the relevant agency to amend reports. Acting early keeps the exposure small.

Where I’d focus first as a payroll administrator

Check your ESD and L&I accounts on a schedule, not only when a notice arrives, and compare each annual notice with what your payroll system actually uses. Automate quarterly reporting wherever you can, and rehearse the no-payroll filing before you need it. Before every run after a rate change, spot-check a few paychecks with a calculator. Small checks catch the errors that become expensive in an audit.

FAQ

What is the Washington payroll tax rate?

Washington has no general state income tax, so there is no single payroll tax rate. Employers pay UI at a rate set by ESD, and Paid Leave is 1.13% of gross wages up to the Social Security cap. WA Cares adds 0.58% for employees.

What taxes come out of my paycheck in Washington state?

Federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare come out, plus the employee share of Paid Leave and WA Cares. No state income tax is withheld. A workers’ compensation share may also apply.

What are the payroll tax rates for Washington in 2026?

The Paid Leave premium is 1.13%, split 71.43% employee and 28.57% employer when the employer share is required, per the ESD announcement. WA Cares is 0.58% and employee-paid. UI rates vary by employer.

How do I file Washington payroll taxes?

Employers file quarterly tax and wage reports with ESD, including no-payroll reports when needed. Due dates fall on the last day of the month after each quarter. Access your accounts through SecureAccess Washington.
