US Employees: Calculate 2026 Take Home Pay and Avoid Paycheck Surprises

Published September 19, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Net pay equals gross pay for the period minus federal withholding, Social Security, Medicare, applicable state and local taxes, and post-tax deductions. To calculate take-home pay, you need six inputs: gross wages (salary or hourly rate plus overtime), pay frequency, your Form W-4 filing status, pretax deductions, your work or resident state, and year-to-date wages. Every legitimate calculation method, including the one behind the Paysums take-home pay calculator, builds from that same sequence.

TL;DR:

  • Social Security withholding stops once year-to-date wages exceed the $184,500 wage base in 2026, but Medicare taxes apply to all taxable income.
  • State and local income taxes vary widely; some states have flat rates, others have graduated brackets, and city taxes can add additional deductions depending on the jurisdiction.
  • Pretax deductions, such as traditional retirement and health premiums, reduce taxable wages before calculating taxes, while post-tax deductions do not affect tax calculations.
  • Using the correct number of pay periods (26 biweekly, 24 semimonthly) is essential for accurate estimates, as over- or underestimating skews net pay calculations.
  • Bonuses under $1 million are typically taxed at a flat 22%, but employers may also combine bonuses with regular wages, which can change the withholding amount.

How to Calculate Take-Home Pay Step by Step

Start with gross pay for the specific pay period, not your annual salary. If you’re salaried, divide annual pay by the number of pay periods (26 for biweekly, 24 for semimonthly). If you’re hourly, multiply your rate by hours worked, then add overtime separately at the higher rate.

From there, work through the deductions in order:

  1. Subtract pretax deductions (401(k), pretax health premiums, HSA contributions) to get taxable wages.
  2. Calculate federal withholding using your Form W-4 filing status and the IRS percentage or wage-bracket method.
  3. Apply Social Security tax at 6.2% of taxable wages, up to the annual wage base.
  4. Apply Medicare tax at 1.45% of all taxable wages, with no cap.
  5. Add state and local income tax if your jurisdiction levies one.
  6. Subtract post-tax deductions (Roth contributions, wage garnishments, union dues).

Here’s a worked example. Say you’re paid $2,000 biweekly with $100 in pretax 401(k) contributions. Taxable wages for federal purposes come to $1,900. No state tax applies. Net pay: $2,000 minus $100 minus $180 minus $117.80 minus $27.55 equals $1,574.65.

Statistic Callout: For 2026, the Social Security taxable maximum sits at $184,500. Once your year-to-date wages cross that line, Social Security withholding stops for the rest of the year, though Medicare keeps applying to every dollar.

What Determines Your Federal Withholding Amount?

Federal withholding is an estimate, not your actual tax bill. Employers calculate it using your wages for that specific payroll period combined with the information on your Form W-4, then run it through IRS percentage or wage-bracket tables to land on a dollar figure.

That estimate can be wrong in either direction. A large refund at tax time means too much was withheld all year, according to the IRS withholding estimator FAQs, which is really an interest-free loan you gave the government. Employers pull their withholding tables directly from IRS Publication 505, and those tables get updated annually.

Signs you should revisit your W-4:

  • You or your spouse started a second job.
  • Your household now has two incomes instead of one.
  • You picked up significant freelance or investment income outside your paycheck.
  • Your family situation changed (marriage, a new dependent, divorce).

Pro Tip: Run the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator any time your income changes mid-year. A five-minute check now beats an unpleasant surprise in April.

How Do Social Security and Medicare Affect Your Paycheck?

FICA taxes are fixed percentages, not brackets. Unlike federal income tax, these rates don’t change based on your filing status or allowances.

Statistic Callout: *The 2026 Social Security wage base is $184,500, meaning workers who earn more than that stop paying Social Security tax on wages above that figure for the rest of the calendar year.

Do You Owe State or Local Income Tax?

Not every paycheck includes a state tax line. A handful of states charge no income tax at all, while others use a flat rate, and many run graduated brackets similar to the federal system. Some cities layer a local tax on top of state withholding, which is common in parts of Ohio, Pennsylvania, and New York.

The rule of thumb: apply the tax rules for the state (and city, if applicable) where the work is actually performed, unless your state has a reciprocity agreement with your state of residence.

  • Flat-rate states withhold the same percentage regardless of income.
  • Graduated-bracket states withhold more as taxable wages rise.
  • City or county taxes stack on top of state withholding in select jurisdictions.
  • Check your state’s department of revenue site directly. State withholding tables change more often than most people expect, and a tool like the take-home pay calculator tracks those updates automatically.

Pretax vs. Post-Tax Deductions: Why Order Matters

Mixing up pretax and post-tax deductions is the single most common calculation mistake, according to CFPB paycheck guidance. Pretax deductions reduce your taxable wages before income tax is calculated; post-tax deductions come out after taxes are already applied and don’t shrink your tax bill at all.

Common pretax items:

  1. Traditional 401(k) or 403(b) contributions.
  2. Health, dental, and vision premiums paid through a cafeteria plan.
  3. HSA and most FSA contributions.

Common post-tax items include Roth 401(k) contributions, wage garnishments, and after-tax life insurance premiums. The correct sequence: subtract pretax deductions first to establish taxable wages, calculate all taxes on that reduced figure, then subtract post-tax deductions from what’s left.

Pro Tip: Pull your benefits plan summary once a year and confirm which deductions are marked pretax versus post-tax. Plan documents sometimes reclassify items during open enrollment, and that quietly changes your taxable wage base.

Why Your Pay Frequency Changes Your Budget Math

Annual salary divided by pay periods only works when you use the actual number of periods, not a rough estimate. Biweekly pay (every two weeks) produces 26 pay periods a year; semimonthly pay (twice a month) produces 24. Those aren’t the same, and confusing them throws off every paycheck estimate that follows.

  • Biweekly: annual salary ÷ 26.
  • Semimonthly: annual salary ÷ 24.
  • Weekly: annual salary ÷ 52.

Statistic Callout: Because 26 pay periods don’t divide evenly into 12 months, biweekly employees get two months a year with three paychecks instead of two. Budgeting off a typical two-paycheck month means that third check should go straight to savings or debt, not routine spending. How Are Bonuses and Overtime Taxed Differently?

Overtime gets added at the gross-pay stage, calculated per workweek under DOL regular-rate rules, then flows through the same withholding math as regular wages.

Bonuses and other supplemental wages work differently:

  • Employers often withhold a flat 22% federal rate on bonuses under $1 million, separate from your regular paycheck withholding.
  • Alternatively, employers can add the bonus to regular wages and withhold as one combined payment (the aggregate method), which sometimes withholds more.
  • Either way, check your pay stub to confirm which method was used before you count on the net amount.

How to Verify Your Paycheck Math Against Your Pay Stub

Your pay stub is the ground truth. CFPB guidance recommends checking these fields, in order:

  1. Pay-period gross wages match your salary or hours calculation.
  2. Taxable wages for federal, state, and FICA match after pretax deductions.
  3. Each withholding line (federal, Social Security, Medicare, state) is present and reasonable.
  4. Year-to-date totals track correctly toward any wage-base cap.
  5. Net pay equals gross minus every deduction line, with nothing missing.

Pro Tip: If your Social Security withholding suddenly drops to zero mid-year, don’t panic. It usually means you crossed the wage base, not a payroll error.

Common Take-Home Pay Calculation Mistakes

The most frequent errors: treating pretax and post-tax deductions as identical, ignoring year-to-date wage-base caps, using an approximate multiplier instead of the exact pay-period count, and confusing gross wages with taxable wages.

  • Fix: separate deductions by tax treatment before you calculate anything.
  • Fix: track YTD wages, especially near the Social Security cap.
  • Fix: always divide by the real number of pay periods, never round.
  • Fix: aim for withholding close to actual liability rather than chasing a big refund.

FAQ

How Can You Calculate Take-Home Pay?

Take gross pay for the period, subtract pretax deductions to get taxable wages, then subtract federal withholding, Social Security, Medicare, applicable state and local tax, and post-tax deductions. The Paysums take-home pay calculator runs this same sequence automatically using 2026 tax tables for your state.

What Is the Formula for Take-Home Salary?

Net pay = gross pay − pretax deductions − federal income tax − Social Security − Medicare − state/local tax − post-tax deductions. Each component depends on your pay frequency, filing status, and jurisdiction, so the dollar amounts vary from paycheck to paycheck even at a fixed salary.

How Do You Figure Out Your Take-Home Salary?

Start with your gross wages for a single pay period, not your annual salary, then work through deductions in order: pretax items first, then taxes, then post-tax items. Using the actual number of pay periods (26 for biweekly, 24 for semimonthly) rather than an estimate keeps the math accurate.

How Do You Calculate What Percentage of Tax Is Taken Out of a Paycheck?

The percentage varies by income level, filing status, and location, since federal tax is graduated while FICA rates are flat up to the Social Security wage base.
