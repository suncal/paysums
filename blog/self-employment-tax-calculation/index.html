Self-Employment Tax Calculation: 2026 Formula and Examples

Published October 22, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Self-employment tax is figured on 92.35% of net self-employment earnings. The 2026 rate is 15.3%, made up of 12.4% Social Security and 2.9% Medicare. The Social Security part stops at a wage base of $184,500. The Medicare part has no cap. This guide gives the formula, three worked examples, the Additional Medicare Tax thresholds and the quarterly payment dates.

TL;DR

  • Formula: net profit × 92.35% = taxable SE earnings. Multiply by 15.3% (12.4% + 2.9%) up to the Social Security cap.
  • 2026 Social Security wage base: $184,500, shared with any W-2 wages. W-2 wages count first.
  • Half of the SE tax is deductible when figuring adjusted gross income (AGI). It does not reduce the SE tax itself.
  • Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% applies above $200,000 (single), $250,000 (married filing jointly) and $125,000 (married filing separately).
  • Estimated payments cover income tax and SE tax together. The remaining 2026 due date is January 15, 2027.

What self-employment tax covers

Employees see Social Security and Medicare withheld from each paycheck. The employer matches the amount. Self-employed workers carry both halves. According to the IRS, self-employment tax is the combined employer and employee shares. That is why the rate is 15.3% instead of the 7.65% an employee sees on a pay stub.

SE tax is separate from federal income tax. A freelancer owes both: income tax on taxable income, and SE tax on net earnings from self-employment. The two are calculated on different bases, which is where most manual estimates go wrong.

The tax applies to sole proprietors, independent contractors, gig workers and partners in a partnership. The IRS says you must file a return if you have net earnings from self-employment of $400 or more, even from a side job.

The 2026 rates and limits

Item 2026 figure
Social Security rate (self-employed) 12.4%
Medicare rate (self-employed) 2.9%
Total SE tax rate 15.3%
Share of net earnings that is taxed 92.35%
Social Security wage base $184,500
Deductible share of SE tax 50%
Additional Medicare Tax 0.9% above the filing-status threshold

The rate and the wage base come from the IRS self-employment tax page. The SSA also lists the 2025 and 2026 wage bases side by side: $176,100 in 2025 and $184,500 in 2026.

The calculation, step by step

  1. Find net profit. Gross business income minus ordinary and necessary business expenses. For a sole proprietor this is the bottom line of Schedule C.
  2. Multiply by 92.35%. This is 100% minus 7.65%, the employer-equivalent share. The result is your taxable SE earnings.
  3. Apply 12.4% Social Security to taxable SE earnings up to $184,500, less any W-2 wages already subject to Social Security tax.
  4. Apply 2.9% Medicare to all taxable SE earnings. There is no cap.
  5. Add the two amounts. This is the SE tax, reported on Schedule SE.
  6. Deduct half of the SE tax on Schedule 1. This lowers AGI and therefore income tax.

The 92.35% step is easy to miss. The IRS lets you figure SE tax on only that share, and estimates that skip it overstate the tax by about 7.65%. The Paysums self-employment tax calculator applies it automatically.

Worked example 1: $80,000 net profit

A freelance designer has $80,000 in net profit and no W-2 job.

Step Calculation Result
Taxable SE earnings $80,000 × 92.35% $73,880.00
Social Security $73,880 × 12.4% $9,161.12
Medicare $73,880 × 2.9% $2,142.52
SE tax $9,161.12 + $2,142.52 $11,303.64
Deductible half $11,303.64 ÷ 2 $5,651.82

The $5,651.82 deduction comes off income, not off the SE tax. At a 22% marginal federal rate it saves roughly $1,243 in income tax. The effective SE tax rate on the original $80,000 is about 14.1%, not 15.3%.

Worked example 2: income above the Social Security cap

A consultant has $200,000 in net profit and no wages.

Step Calculation Result
Taxable SE earnings $200,000 × 92.35% $184,700.00
Social Security $184,500 (cap) × 12.4% $22,878.00
Medicare $184,700 × 2.9% $5,356.30
SE tax $28,234.30
Deductible half $14,117.15

Taxable SE earnings of $184,700 exceed the cap by $200, so Social Security tax is computed on $184,500 only. Medicare applies to the full $184,700. Every extra dollar of profit from here carries only the 2.9% Medicare rate (plus the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax once thresholds are crossed).

Worked example 3: W-2 wages plus self-employment

Many people have a day job and a side business. The SSA explains that the wage base applies to W-2 wages first. If wages already reach $184,500, no Social Security tax is owed on the SE income. Medicare still applies to all net self-employment earnings.

Suppose a worker earns $150,000 in W-2 wages and has $60,000 in net profit from a side business.

Step Calculation Result
Taxable SE earnings $60,000 × 92.35% $55,410.00
Wage base remaining $184,500 − $150,000 $34,500.00
Social Security $34,500 × 12.4% $4,278.00
Medicare $55,410 × 2.9% $1,606.89
SE tax $5,884.89

The worker still owes the full Medicare portion. Only the Social Security portion is partly shielded. Worth noting: this example shows the base mechanics only. The Schedule SE worksheet contains the official line-by-line version, and a W-2 employer’s withholding on the $150,000 does not change the SE figure above.

Additional Medicare Tax

A 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax applies to Medicare wages and self-employment income above these thresholds:

Filing status Threshold
Single, head of household $200,000
Married filing jointly $250,000
Married filing separately $125,000

The thresholds are not indexed for inflation. With both wages and SE income, the SE threshold is reduced by the Medicare wages received. Unlike regular SE tax, there is no deduction for half of this tax. Form 8959 is used to figure it, as the IRS form instructions describe.

The IRS also notes that you cannot direct estimated payments to this tax alone. Payments go against total liability, so anyone who expects to cross a threshold should raise the total estimated payments to cover it.

Paying quarterly: due dates and safe amounts

Employers withhold. The self-employed must pay as they earn. The IRS says estimated tax is generally required if you expect to owe $1,000 or more when you file. Late or short payments can trigger an underpayment penalty.

The 2026 dates for Form 1040-ES are:

Payment Due date
Q1 April 15, 2026
Q2 June 15, 2026
Q3 September 15, 2026
Q4 January 15, 2027

As of October 20, 2026, three of the four dates have passed. If an earlier payment was missed or was too small, the Q4 payment on January 15, 2027 can still be sized to reduce the shortfall, though a penalty for the earlier quarters may still apply. The 2026 Form 1040-ES package contains the worksheet and vouchers.

Each payment should cover income tax and SE tax together. A simple method: estimate annual net profit, run the SE tax calculation above, add income tax on the remaining taxable income, then divide by four. Our walkthrough on paying quarterly estimated taxes covers the process in detail.

Businesses that also run payroll face a separate quarterly calendar. Employer filings such as Form 941 follow their own schedule, outlined in this guide to Form 941 due dates for employers. Those dates do not replace the 1040-ES dates for the owner’s own income.

Ways to lower the base

SE tax is figured on net profit, so legitimate business expenses reduce it directly. Retirement contributions work differently: they reduce income tax but generally not SE tax.

  • Business expenses: every deductible dollar cuts net profit and therefore the SE tax by about 14.1 cents.
  • Half of SE tax: deducted on Schedule 1 for income tax purposes.
  • Retirement plans: a solo 401(k), SEP IRA or SIMPLE IRA defers income from current income tax. The SSA lists the 2026 elective deferral limit for 401(k) plans at $24,500 and the defined contribution limit at $72,000. Plan rules vary, so confirm your own limit with the plan provider or a tax professional.
  • Entity structure: some owners consider an S corporation election at higher profit levels. This changes payroll and filing duties and needs advice from a CPA or enrolled agent before acting.

W-2 or 1099: how the tax differs

The same $80,000 earned as a W-2 employee costs 7.65% in payroll tax, or $6,120. As a 1099 contractor the SE tax is $11,303.64 before the half deduction. Contractors therefore need a higher rate to match an employee’s take-home pay. The W-2 vs 1099 calculator shows the equivalent rate, and the article on W-2 vs 1099 taxes explains where the gap comes from.

Worker classification is not a free choice. For a plain-language overview of the tests, see this explainer on how to tell a contractor from an employee.

Our view: Perspective

We see the same three errors in self-employment estimates. First, people apply 15.3% to gross income, not to 92.35% of net profit. Second, they treat the deductible half as a credit against SE tax, when it only lowers income tax. Third, they plan for the annual total but not for the cash timing, so the January bill arrives as a surprise.

Our approach is to show each line of the calculation, so a user can compare it with Schedule SE. A single “you owe” number hides the steps, and the steps are what let a freelancer see how a purchase, a retirement contribution or a rate change moves the result. We keep the tables current because a stale wage base or threshold produces a wrong answer that looks right.

Self-employment tax is not optional and not negotiable, but it is predictable. Once net profit is estimated, the rest is arithmetic.

FAQ

What percentage of tax should I pay on self-employment income?

The SE tax rate is 15.3% of 92.35% of net earnings, which is about 14.1% of net profit below the Social Security cap. Federal income tax, and any state income tax, come on top. Income tax depends on filing status, deductions and total taxable income.

Do I owe self-employment tax if I also have a W-2 job?

Yes, if your net self-employment earnings are $400 or more. Your W-2 wages count toward the $184,500 Social Security wage base first. If wages reach the base, only the 2.9% Medicare portion applies to the SE income.

Can I deduct self-employment tax?

You can deduct half of it when figuring AGI, on Schedule 1. The deduction lowers income tax. It does not reduce the SE tax itself or the Additional Medicare Tax.

When do I have to make estimated payments?

The IRS generally expects quarterly payments if you expect to owe $1,000 or more when you file. The 2026 dates are April 15, June 15 and September 15, 2026, and January 15, 2027. Payments cover income tax and SE tax combined.

Does the 15.3% rate change by state?

No. SE tax is a federal tax at the same rate in every state. States may add their own income tax, which the state paycheck calculator covers for all 50 states and DC.
