Retro Pay Calculation: Formulas, Examples and Tax Withholding

Published October 20, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Retro pay equals the difference between what an employee should have been paid and what was actually paid, multiplied by the hours or pay periods affected. The gross amount is then taxed as supplemental wages in the year it is paid. This guide covers the formula for hourly and salaried workers, overtime effects, withholding, and the cases where back pay is treated differently.

TL;DR

  • Gross retro pay = (correct rate − rate paid) × affected hours. For salary, divide the annual raise by the number of pay periods, then multiply by the periods missed.
  • Overtime hours need the rate difference multiplied by the overtime multiplier, because the regular rate of pay for past weeks changes.
  • Tax: retro pay is generally supplemental wages. Federal withholding is either the optional 22% flat rate (if paid separately) or the aggregate method.
  • Social Security and Medicare apply in full, up to the annual wage base.
  • Court- or agency-ordered back pay can follow different year-allocation rules.

What retro pay is

Retroactive pay is compensation owed for work already performed. It arises when a raise is approved with an earlier effective date, a payroll error underpaid an employee, a union contract is ratified late, or a promotion takes effect before the system is updated. Back pay is the broader term. The U.S. Department of Labor describes it as the difference between what an employee was paid and what should have been paid, often ordered as a remedy for wage violations.

In practice, the two terms describe the same arithmetic. The distinction matters for taxes and reporting, covered below.

The core formula

Every retro pay calculation follows four steps:

  1. Identify the effective date of the correct rate and the date the new rate actually started in payroll.
  2. Find the rate difference (new rate minus old rate).
  3. Count the affected hours or pay periods between those dates.
  4. Multiply the difference by the affected hours or periods.
Pay type Formula
Hourly, no overtime (new rate − old rate) × hours worked
Hourly, with overtime (rate difference × regular hours) + (rate difference × overtime hours × overtime multiplier)
Salaried (new annual salary − old annual salary) ÷ pay periods per year × periods affected
Underpayment error corrected gross pay − gross pay actually paid, per pay period, summed

Worked example: hourly employee with overtime

An employee was paid $24.00 per hour, but a raise to $25.50 was effective for the last 6 weeks (a total of 120 regular hours and 10 overtime hours at time and a half).

Component Calculation Amount
Rate difference $25.50 − $24.00 $1.50
Regular hours $1.50 × 120 $180.00
Overtime hours $1.50 × 1.5 × 10 $22.50
Gross retro pay $202.50

The overtime line is the one most calculations miss. Hours already paid at 1.5 times the old rate were underpaid by 1.5 times the difference, not by the difference alone.

Worked example: salaried employee

A salary rises from $58,000 to $61,480, effective six bi-weekly pay periods before payroll processed it.

  • Annual difference: $61,480 − $58,000 = $3,480
  • Per bi-weekly period (26 per year): $3,480 ÷ 26 = $133.85
  • Six periods: $133.85 × 6 = $803.08 gross (using the unrounded figure, $803.08)

For semi-monthly pay, divide by 24 and multiply by the semi-monthly periods missed. The bi-weekly paycheck calculator shows the regular net pay under the new salary, so the retro check can be compared against it.

Overtime and the regular rate of pay

For non-exempt employees, a retroactive raise is not a stand-alone bonus. Because it pays for past work, it belongs in the regular rate for the weeks it covers. The DOL Field Operations Handbook, Chapter 32 explains how the regular rate is computed from total remuneration for hours worked in the workweek.

The working method:

  1. Allocate the raise to the workweeks in which the work was performed.
  2. Recompute the regular rate for each of those weeks.
  3. Pay the extra half-time premium on overtime hours at the new regular rate.

When allocation by week is not practical, the usual fallback is to divide the total retro amount by total hours worked in the period, to get an equitable hourly increase. Employers should confirm the approach with a payroll professional or employment counsel before relying on it.

Overtime errors have their own formulas. See the guide on how to calculate overtime pay and the piece on shift differential pay checks, since differentials also enter the regular rate.

When the overtime cannot be computed on time

Some payrolls pay overtime late because the amount cannot be determined before the regular payday. Guidance from the DOL says the delay cannot exceed the time reasonably necessary to compute the amount, and the payment is due no later than the next payday after the calculation is complete. Employees in California facing late wage or overtime payments can consult an employment attorney, such as the team at Soho Law Firm, about state-specific deadlines.

How retro pay is taxed

Federal income tax withholding

The IRS generally treats retroactive pay as supplemental wages. Under Publication 15, an employer may use a flat 22% rate on supplemental wages when they are identified separately from regular wages. A mandatory 37% rate applies once an employee’s supplemental wages for the year exceed $1 million. The alternative is the aggregate method: combine the retro payment with regular wages for the period and withhold as if it were one payment.

Method How it works Typical effect
Flat 22% Retro pay listed separately, 22% withheld Predictable, can exceed or fall short of actual liability
Aggregate Retro added to regular wages, tax computed on the total Larger one-time withholding, tied to W-4 and bracket

Withholding is not the final tax. The actual liability is settled on the annual return, so over-withheld tax comes back as a refund and under-withheld tax is owed. The client’s guide, 22% vs aggregate: how back pay net changes, compares both methods on sample paychecks, and the bonus tax rate guide covers the same supplemental wage rules.

Social Security and Medicare

Retro pay is subject to FICA like any other wages. Employee Social Security tax is 6.2% and Medicare is 1.45%. The Social Security wage base is $184,500 for 2026, so a large retro payment to a high earner may be partly or fully above the cap. More detail is in the Social Security tax guide.

Estimated net on the $202.50 example

This illustration uses the flat 22% method and ignores state and local tax:

Item Calculation Amount
Gross retro pay $202.50
Federal income tax (22%) $202.50 × 0.22 −$44.55
Social Security (6.2%) $202.50 × 0.062 −$12.56
Medicare (1.45%) $202.50 × 0.0145 −$2.94
Estimated net $142.45

State income tax, pre-tax deductions and 401(k) elections change this figure. The take-home pay calculator applies the state and filing status inputs. Retro pay that is also subject to state supplemental rates, as in California, is discussed in the California withholding guide.

Which year the money belongs to

For ordinary retro pay, the rule is simple. IRS Publication 15-A says to treat back pay as wages in the year paid. A raise approved in January for work done in November is a current-year wage.

The exception is back pay awarded by a court or government agency to enforce a federal or state statute protecting employment rights. In that case the employer may need to report the wages to the Social Security Administration, with the allocation to earlier years, using Form SSA-131. Publication 957 explains that for Social Security coverage and benefit purposes, back pay is generally attributed to the earlier years it relates to. Federal retirement reporting follows a similar split: OPM’s reporting guide separates statutory back pay awards from other retroactive adjustments.

Situation Generally treated as
Negotiated or delayed raise Wages in the year paid
Payroll error correction Wages in the year paid
Statutory back pay (FLSA, NLRA and similar) May be credited to prior years for Social Security reporting

Deadlines, interest and recovery

Three time limits and add-ons matter in disputes:

  • Limitation period. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, claims for back wages generally run two years, or three for willful violations. Liquidated damages equal to the unpaid amount, attorney’s fees and court costs can be added in litigation.
  • Interest. Private-sector retro pay is usually paid without interest unless a contract, court order or state law requires it. Federal employees’ back pay carries interest at IRS quarterly rates. OPM’s rate table lists 7% annual for the quarter beginning October 1, 2026.
  • Overpayments. In the other direction, DOL guidance says employers may recoup an inadvertent overpayment from a later paycheck, provided the deduction does not cut pay below minimum wage or overtime requirements. State law can add limits.

Household and prior-period payroll corrections

Household employers such as families paying a nanny face the same arithmetic: a missed raise or an unpaid overtime week needs a rate difference and a count of affected hours. A service for cleaning up prior wage reporting and household payroll can help when several periods and caregivers need to be reconciled. The nanny tax (Schedule H) tools cover the employer tax side.

Our perspective

Most retro pay errors come from three inputs: the wrong start date, ignoring overtime hours, and treating the gross amount as the take-home figure. We update our tax tables weekly from IRS, SSA and state sources, so the withholding estimate for a retro check reflects the current 22% flat rate, FICA rates and state rules rather than a rule of thumb.

We recommend calculating retro pay in two passes. First compute gross by pay period in a spreadsheet, so each period is auditable. Second, run the total as a separate supplemental payment and as part of regular wages, and compare net results. The difference is usually a withholding timing matter, not a change in annual tax. Employees should check that the retro payment appears as its own line on the stub, and employers should keep the calculation worksheet with payroll records.

Retro pay checklist

  • Confirm the written effective date of the raise or correction.
  • List each pay period affected, with regular and overtime hours.
  • Compute the rate difference, with the overtime multiplier applied.
  • Choose and document the withholding method (flat 22% or aggregate).
  • Add FICA and state taxes.
  • Verify the payment on the stub and keep the worksheet.

FAQ

Is retro pay taxed at a higher rate?

Not as a separate tax rate. It is taxed as ordinary income, but withholding can look higher because of the 22% flat rate or because the aggregate method pushes one paycheck into a higher withholding bracket. The actual tax is reconciled on the annual return.

How do I calculate backdated pay?

Subtract the old rate from the correct rate, then multiply by the hours or pay periods affected. Add the overtime premium on overtime hours. Then subtract tax and deductions to estimate net pay.

How do I calculate back pay for an underpayment?

Compare the gross pay that should have been paid with the gross pay actually paid for each period, and add the differences. If a wage claim is involved, the FLSA limitation period is generally two or three years, so check which periods are within reach.

Can retro pay be calculated in Excel?

Yes. Use one row per pay period with columns for old rate, new rate, regular hours, overtime hours and multiplier. The formula is (new − old) × (regular hours + overtime hours × multiplier), summed at the bottom.
