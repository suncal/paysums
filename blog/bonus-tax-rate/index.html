Calculate Your 2026 Bonus Net: 22% Withholding and Missouri Examples

Published October 7, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

The 2026 federal supplemental withholding rate on bonuses is 22%, rising to 37% on supplemental wages above $1,000,000 in a calendar year. Social Security withholding of 6.2% and Medicare withholding of 1.45% still apply, and Missouri employers commonly use a flat supplemental rate for state withholding. None of these figures are your final tax bill: withholding is only an estimate that gets reconciled when you file.

TL;DR:

  • Employers generally withhold 22% on separately paid bonuses, while aggregate withholding combines the bonus with regular wages and may produce a higher initial deduction.
  • Social Security takes 6.2% only until annual wages reach $184,500, but Medicare’s 1.45% has no cap, so bonus timing can change take home pay.
  • Missouri employers may use flat 4.7% or aggregate state withholding; compare both estimates and adjust your state form if projected withholding falls short.

How federal withholding on bonuses works in 2026

Employers withhold tax on bonuses using one of two IRS methods; for companies considering international hiring options, compliant Portuguese employment contracts and payroll administration can be managed seamlessly through specialized services in Portugal, allowing foreign employers to navigate bonus withholding rules with ease, as explained at Portugal Christmas Bonus. The flat 22% supplemental rate applies when a bonus is paid separately from regular wages, while the aggregate method combines the bonus with a regular paycheck and withholds based on the total using standard tax tables, as described in Publication 15 (2026), Employer’s Tax Guide. Most employers choose the flat rate because it is simpler to administer.

The 22% rate is only a withholding method, not your tax bracket. Publication 505 confirms that your actual tax liability depends on total annual income and filing status, settled on Form 1040. If too much was withheld, you get a refund; if too little, you owe the difference.

A few rules shape the math:

  • Supplemental wages above $1,000,000 in a calendar year face mandatory 37% federal withholding on the excess.
  • The 0.9% Additional Medicare tax kicks in once Medicare wages exceed $200,000. Employers must withhold it automatically.
  • Aggregate withholding can push some or all of a bonus into a higher marginal bracket than the flat 22% rate would suggest.

FICA and the 2026 wage base: how Social Security and Medicare affect bonus withholding

Bonuses count as wages for Social Security and Medicare purposes, so FICA withholding applies the same way it does to regular pay. The 2026 Social Security wage base is $184,500, and employees pay 6.2% on earnings up to that cap. Medicare withholding is 1.45% with no wage ceiling, plus an extra 0.9% once Medicare wages for the year cross $200,000.

Once your year-to-date wages pass $184,500, Social Security withholding on a bonus drops to zero. That single mechanic explains why a December bonus can sometimes net out higher than an identical bonus paid in January.

Key mechanics to watch:

  • Social Security withholding stops entirely once the $184,500 cap is reached for the year.
  • Medicare withholding never stops, regardless of total income.
  • The Additional Medicare tax is an employer withholding obligation, not optional, once the $200,000 threshold is crossed.

Missouri specifics: 2026 supplemental withholding and your next steps

Which method your employer uses can change your paycheck meaningfully on a large bonus.

A few practical points for Missouri employees:

  • Ask payroll which method they use: flat 4.7% or aggregate, since the two can produce different take-home amounts.
  • If withholding looks too low relative to your expected annual liability, submit a new MO W-4 to adjust it.
  • Compare your Missouri withholding against last year’s state tax return to catch under-withholding early.

Pro Tip: Run your bonus through a 2026-updated calculator under both flat and aggregate assumptions before assuming your paycheck reflects your final state tax.

How to estimate your after-tax bonus step by step

Before running any bonus calculation, gather these inputs:

  • Gross bonus amount
  • Year-to-date wages
  • Filing status
  • State of residence
  • Pre-tax deferrals (401(k), HSA, etc.)
  • Whether your employer uses flat supplemental or aggregate withholding
  • Federal and state tax already withheld this year

Once you have those, follow this sequence:

  1. Identify the withholding method your employer uses for the bonus.
  2. Apply federal withholding: 22% flat, or your marginal rate if aggregate.
  3. Add FICA, checking whether year-to-date wages have crossed the Social Security wage base.
  4. Apply your state’s supplemental or aggregate withholding rule.
  5. Subtract pre-tax deferrals from the gross bonus before calculating taxable wages.
  6. Compare the result to your expected net bonus.

Pro Tip: Test edge cases deliberately: a bonus that pushes you over the Social Security cap, over the $200,000 Medicare threshold, or near the $1,000,000 supplemental wage mark will behave differently than a routine bonus.

Worked examples: three bonus scenarios with full math

Example A: a $1,000 bonus. Federal withholding at 22% removes $220. Net bonus: roughly $656.50.

Example B: a $20,000 bonus. Flat federal withholding takes $4,400. FICA removes $1,240 (Social Security) and $290 (Medicare), assuming the wage base has not been reached. Net under the flat method: about $13,130. Under the aggregate method, if the bonus pushes the employee into the 24% federal bracket, withholding could run higher, meaning a smaller paycheck now but a closer match to actual liability at filing, per Publication 505 guidance on reconciliation.

Example C: supplemental wages exceeding $1,000,000. Per Publication 15 (2026), amounts over $1,000,000 in a calendar year are withheld at 37% federal, not 22%. Medicare’s 1.45% applies with no cap, and the Additional Medicare tax of 0.9% applies to wages above $200,000. Social Security withholding stops once the $184,500 wage base is reached. At filing, high earners often reconcile a meaningful gap between withheld amounts and actual liability depending on deductions and other income.

Order of operations matters:

  1. Determine which portion of the bonus falls under 22% versus 37% withholding.
  2. Apply FICA based on year-to-date wages, not the bonus alone.
  3. Apply state supplemental withholding.
  4. Compare total withheld to your expected tax bracket for reconciliation planning.

What Missouri bonus earners often get wrong

A bonus does not get taxed more than regular income; it just gets withheld differently, often at a flat percentage that ignores your deductions, dependents, or other income.

The practical fix is simple: check your year-to-date wages, run a current calculator, and adjust your W-4 or MO W-4 if withholding looks mismatched to your expected liability. For anyone juggling multiple jobs, freelance income, or large equity payouts, a tax professional is worth the conversation.
