Pay Stub Laws by State: Your Rights and How to Rebuild Missing Records

Published October 8, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Federal law does not require employers to give you a pay stub. The FLSA requires accurate payroll records but stops short of mandating a pay statement for employees, so your actual rights come from your state’s labor code. Check your state labor department’s guidance, and keep your own pay records regardless of what your employer provides.

TL;DR:

  • Federal law requires employers to retain payroll records for three years and time cards for two, but not to issue routine statements.
  • California, New York, and Minnesota require itemized statements each pay period, though required fields vary; check your state labor department for local rules.
  • Electronic statements count where allowed only if you can access them independently and print or export them; save copies before leaving a job.
  • If a statement is missing or wrong, request it in writing, preserve deposit records and time logs, then contact your state labor department if unresolved.

Federal baseline: what the FLSA actually requires

The Fair Labor Standards Act sets the floor, and that floor is about employer recordkeeping, not employee notification. According to the Department of Labor’s elaws advisor, the FLSA does not require employers to furnish pay stubs to employees at all. It only obligates employers to maintain accurate wage and hour records internally.

That recordkeeping duty still has teeth. Under the same DOL guidance:

  • Payroll records must be kept for at least three years.
  • Records used for wage computations, including time cards and work schedules, must be kept for at least two years.
  • None of these records are required to be shared with employees as a routine pay stub.

Because federal law sets only this minimum, states are free to go further, and many have. Whether you actually receive an itemized pay stub each payday depends entirely on where you work.

Does your state require pay stubs? Here’s how the rules differ

State law is where the real answer lives, and it splits roughly into three camps: states that require a detailed, itemized statement every pay period, states that allow access without a strict paper requirement, and states with no explicit mandate at all. Knowing which camp your state falls into tells you what you can actually expect from your employer.

California sets one of the strictest standards. Labor Code Section 226 requires an accurate itemized statement with every payment of wages, listing nine specific items, and the California DLSE opinion letter confirms employers must keep copies of these statements for at least three years. New York takes a similar approach. The New York Department of Labor’s wage statement guidance requires employers to list gross and net wages, hours worked, rates of pay, deductions, and employer information on every statement, and employees can request a written explanation of how their wages were calculated.

Minnesota rounds out the group of states with detailed mandates. The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry requires a written earnings statement at the end of every pay period covering ten specific fields, and employers must retain copies for three years.

A few practical points apply no matter where you live:

  • States without an explicit pay-stub statute still generally require employers to maintain internal payroll records that you can request to see.
  • Even in states with strong itemization rules, the exact required fields differ, so a stub that satisfies Minnesota’s checklist won’t automatically satisfy California’s.
  • Your state labor department’s website is the fastest way to confirm what applies to you, and calling their wage-and-hour division clears up edge cases that general guides can’t.

What should actually be on your pay stub

When a state does require a wage statement, the required fields exist so you can verify your pay is correct, not just as paperwork. Here’s what to expect on a compliant stub:

  1. Your name or employee identification number.
  2. The pay period start and end dates.
  3. Gross pay for the period.
  4. Total hours worked, including any overtime hours at the applicable rate.
  5. Hourly rate or rates, including overtime and any piece-rate or shift-differential figures.
  6. Each deduction itemized separately, including taxes, benefits, and garnishments.
  7. Net pay after all deductions.
  8. Employer’s legal name, address, and phone number.

Some states tack on extras tied to their own labor rules. Prevailing-wage jobs might show a separate fringe-benefit line, and piece-rate workers often see a distinct earnings category for piece pay versus other compensable time. If any of these fields are missing, blank, or don’t match your expected hours and rate, that’s a signal worth following up on rather than ignoring.

When a digital pay stub meets the legal bar

Electronic pay statements are common, and most states that mandate wage statements allow them, but only under specific conditions. The core requirement is employee control: you need to be able to access the statement independently, and you need the ability to print or export it rather than relying solely on employer login access that could disappear if you change jobs.

The California DLSE opinion letter laid out these conditions years ago, and states like Maryland have published similar guidance confirming that electronic delivery works as long as workers retain a way to convert the statement to a hard copy.

A few habits protect you either way. Download each pay stub as a PDF when it’s issued. Save screenshots with visible timestamps if a portal doesn’t allow downloads. Keep a running record of bank deposits that correspond to each pay period, since that record exists independently of any employer system.

What to do if your pay stub is missing or wrong

If a pay stub never arrives, or the numbers don’t add up, a clear sequence gets you answers faster than guessing.

  1. Put your request in writing to payroll or HR, asking for the pay stub or a written explanation of how your wages were computed, and keep a copy of that request.
  2. Gather supporting evidence: bank deposit records, time logs, and any payroll emails, then use a paycheck calculator to estimate what your net pay should have been.
  3. If the issue isn’t resolved, file a complaint with your state labor department or consult an employment attorney. Many states attach penalties to wage-statement violations, so note the dates involved.

Most states give you a direct complaint channel, and some count missing or defective statements as a basis for statutory penalties, so documentation at each step matters.

How long records must be kept, and the separate W-2 rule

Federal recordkeeping rules require employers to hold payroll records for at least three years and wage-computation records like time cards for at least two, per the DOL’s FLSA guidance. States often set their own retention periods on top of that, with Minnesota requiring earnings statements to be kept for three years.

Separately, the IRS requires every employer to issue Form W-2 by January 31 following the tax year, regardless of what state pay-stub law applies. If a W-2 never arrives, the IRS can help you request a substitute. None of this replaces keeping your own copies: employer records can be lost, destroyed, or simply unavailable when you need them most.

Why pay transparency matters more than people assume

Most people only notice their pay stub is missing or wrong when something else goes sideways: a mortgage application, a tax filing, a dispute over unpaid overtime. By then, reconstructing months of pay history from memory is nearly impossible. We think the real lesson isn’t that pay stubs are legally required everywhere, because they aren’t. It’s that waiting until you need the proof is the wrong time to start keeping it.

FAQ

Can a job refuse to give you pay stubs?

It depends on your state. In states without a wage-statement law, there’s no explicit requirement for your employer to hand over a pay stub, though they must still keep payroll records internally under the FLSA. In states like California, New York, or Minnesota, refusing to provide an itemized statement each pay period can violate state law.

Do I really need to keep my pay stubs?

Yes, because employer records can be incomplete, lost, or simply inaccessible after you leave a job. Keeping your own copies protects you for tax filing, loan applications, and wage disputes, and backs up the retention periods your employer is already required to follow.

What can I use if I don’t have a pay stub?

Bank deposit records, time logs, and payroll emails can reconstruct your pay history, and your Form W-2 confirms annual totals. A paycheck calculator can also help you estimate what your net pay should have been for a given period.

Is it illegal to not see your pay stubs?

In states that mandate itemized wage statements, such as California, New York, and Minnesota, failing to provide one can violate state labor law. In states without such a mandate, there’s no specific illegality, though you generally retain the right to request wage computation details from your employer.
