U.S. Commission Taxes: Model Your Take Home with 22% and 15.3%

Published October 5, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Commissions are taxable as ordinary income no matter how you’re classified. If you’re an employee, your employer typically withholds income tax plus Social Security and Medicare, often using supplemental wage rules such as the optional 22% flat rate. If you’re an independent contractor, you receive the gross commission and owe income tax plus self-employment tax, paid through quarterly estimated payments.

TL;DR:

  • Commissions are taxable income for both employees and independent contractors, with employees typically having taxes withheld automatically.
  • Employers can choose between aggregate or flat 22% percentage withholding methods for supplemental wages, affecting how much is taken upfront.
  • Social Security and Medicare taxes are deducted from employees’ commissions until the wage base is reached, with additional Medicare taxes applying after $200,000 in wages.
  • Contractors must calculate and pay self-employment tax at 15.3% and estimate quarterly taxes, setting aside roughly 25% to 30% of their commissions.
  • Accurately modeling take-home pay using specific calculators helps avoid surprises at tax time, especially for contractors managing self-employment taxes and estimated payments.

How withholding on commissions works

Commissions count as supplemental wages under IRS rules, a category that also includes bonuses and overtime. Publication 15 lays out two withholding methods employers can choose between:

  • Aggregate method: your commission is combined with your regular wages for that pay period, and withholding is calculated on the total using your W-4 elections.
  • Percentage method: your employer withholds a flat 22% from the commission amount, separate from your regular paycheck’s withholding.

The flat rate climbs to 37% on any portion of supplemental wages that pushes your total supplemental pay over $1 million in a calendar year, a threshold that affects very few commission earners but matters for high-volume sales roles with large bonus structures.

The 22% figure is a withholding method, not your actual tax bill. Your real liability depends on your total annual income and bracket, which is why many commission-heavy employees see a refund or a balance due when they file, even though the withholding looked correct on each check.

State withholding rules vary, and some states apply their own supplemental rate or require the aggregate method only. Backup withholding, a separate 24% rate, applies in limited cases such as when a payer lacks your correct taxpayer identification number, and it is distinct from supplemental wage withholding.

To figure out which method your employer used, compare the federal withholding line on a commission-only check against your regular paycheck. A flat 22% deduction with no reference to your W-4 allowances signals the percentage method; a withholding amount that shifts with your filing status points to the aggregate method.

Payroll taxes that reduce your commission net pay

Beyond income tax withholding, every commission paycheck for an employee carries Social Security and Medicare deductions, known together as FICA.

Tax Employee rate Wage base or threshold
Social Security 6.2% Up to the 2026 wage base of $184,500
Medicare 1.45% No wage base, applies to all wages
Additional Medicare Tax 0.9% Wages above $200,000

Your employer matches the Social Security and Medicare amounts, but that employer share never appears on your pay stub as a deduction from your pay. Once your year-to-date wages cross the Social Security wage base, Social Security withholding stops for the rest of the year, which can make a late-year commission check noticeably larger in net terms than an earlier one of the same gross amount.

The Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% kicks in only after your wages for the year exceed $200,000, and it applies regardless of filing status at the withholding stage, even though your actual liability at filing depends on your combined household income.

Pro Tip: Check the year-to-date Social Security wages box on your pay stub each pay period to see how close you are to the wage base, since that number predicts when your take-home jumps.

Employee vs independent contractor: why classification matters

How your commission is taxed depends entirely on whether you’re classified as an employee or an independent contractor, and that classification is based on the working relationship, not your job title. The IRS worker classification guidance centers on behavioral control, financial control, and the nature of the relationship between you and the payer.

  • Employee: your employer controls how, when, and where you work, withholds income tax and FICA from each check, and issues a W-2 at year-end.
  • Independent contractor: you control your own methods and schedule, receive gross pay with no withholding, and get a 1099-NEC if the payer meets the reporting threshold.

A smaller group of sales workers fall into a statutory employee category, which includes certain full-time life insurance salespeople, agent-drivers, and traveling salespeople who meet specific IRS tests. IRS guidance on this classification explains that statutory employees have Social Security and Medicare withheld like regular employees but may still deduct business expenses the way contractors do, which changes how commission income is reported on their tax return.

If your classification is unclear, a few concrete steps help:

  • Ask payroll or HR directly whether you’re classified as a W-2 employee or a 1099 contractor for the commission portion of your pay.
  • Request a copy of your written agreement or offer letter, which often states the classification explicitly.
  • Keep records of who sets your schedule, supplies your tools, and directs your sales process, since those facts determine the correct classification if a dispute arises.

Misclassification carries real consequences: a worker treated as a contractor who should have been an employee may owe self-employment tax on income that should have had FICA withheld, and reconciling that difference later is far harder than confirming classification up front.

Self-employment tax and estimated payments for commission-based contractors

If you earn commissions as an independent contractor, you report that income on Schedule C and calculate your self-employment tax on Schedule SE. The self-employed individuals tax center confirms that self-employment tax combines both the employee and employer shares of Social Security and Medicare into a single 15.3% rate, since no employer is withholding a matching portion on your behalf.

A widely cited rule of thumb: setting aside roughly 25% to 30% of each commission check covers federal income tax and the 15.3% self-employment tax for many contractors, though your actual rate depends on your bracket and deductions.

Because no one withholds taxes from contractor pay, the estimated taxes guidance requires quarterly payments using Form 1040-ES. A practical approach for each commission check:

  1. Set aside 15.3% immediately for self-employment tax, since that portion is nearly fixed regardless of your bracket.
  2. Add an estimated federal income tax percentage based on your marginal bracket, often an additional 10% to 22% depending on total income.
  3. Reserve a separate amount for state income tax if your state imposes one, since rates and rules vary by state.
  4. Pay the accumulated reserve to the IRS by the quarterly due date rather than waiting until filing season.

Safe-harbor rules reduce penalty risk: paying at least 90% of the current year’s tax or 100% of the prior year’s tax liability through the year, as described in Publication 505, generally avoids an underpayment penalty. If your commission income swings widely by quarter, annualizing your income on Form 2210 can lower the penalty compared to assuming even payments. For a contractor with irregular commission income, running the numbers through a 1099 tax calculator before each quarterly deadline catches shortfalls before they become penalties.

Reporting and forms: W-2, 1099-NEC, and year-end reconciliation

Which form reports your commission depends on your classification. Employees see commissions folded into Box 1 wages on a W-2, alongside the income tax, Social Security, and Medicare already withheld. Independent contractors instead receive a 1099-NEC from each payer that paid them nonemployee compensation above the reporting threshold, per the instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC.

A few less common cases change the picture:

  • Direct sellers and certain door-to-door sales agents may receive different reporting depending on whether they’re treated as statutory employees or independent contractors.
  • Insurance and real estate agents often work as statutory nonemployees, a distinct category with its own reporting rules.
  • A payer that issues the wrong form, a 1099-NEC instead of a W-2, or vice versa, should be contacted directly to correct the error before you file.

At year-end, reconciliation means matching every W-2 and 1099-NEC you received against your own commission records. A missing or incorrect form delays filing and can trigger an IRS notice if the income on file doesn’t match what the payer reported. Contact the payer first; if they don’t correct it, the IRS provides a process for reporting the discrepancy directly on your return.

Worked examples: commission gross to net

The gap between gross commission and take-home pay looks very different depending on classification.

Example A: $5,000 commission, W-2 employee, flat 22% method

  1. Federal withholding at the supplemental rate: $5,000 x 22% = $1,100.
  2. Social Security: $5,000 x 6.2% = $310 (assuming the wage base hasn’t been reached).
  3. Medicare: $5,000 x 1.45% = $72.50.
  4. Approximate net before state tax and pre-tax deductions: $5,000 minus $1,100 minus $310 minus $72.50 = $3,517.

Example B: $5,000 commission, independent contractor

  1. Self-employment tax: $5,000 x 15.3% = $765.
  2. Estimated federal income tax reserve at a 15% marginal assumption: $5,000 x 15% = $750.
  3. Amount to set aside: $765 plus $750 = $1,515.
  4. Approximate net available after reserving for taxes: $5,000 minus $1,515 = $3,485.
Scenario Gross Taxes withheld or reserved Approximate net
Employee, 22% flat method $5,000 $1,482 $3,517
Contractor, SE tax plus income tax reserve $5,000 $1,515 $3,485

Both examples ignore state tax and pre-tax deductions, which shift the final number in either direction. The employee’s $1,100 federal withholding is not final: at filing, it reconciles against actual tax owed and can produce a refund or a balance due. The contractor’s reserve is an estimate, not a payment, until it’s actually sent in with Form 1040-ES. Running either scenario through a take-home pay calculator with your actual state and filing status replaces these approximations with your specific numbers.

Checklist: what to check each pay period

A quick review of each commission check catches problems before they compound at filing time.

  • Confirm the commission appears as a separate line from regular wages, or combined per the aggregate method, so you know which withholding rule applied.
  • Check federal withholding against the 22% flat rate or your expected aggregate amount.
  • Verify Social Security and Medicare were withheld at 6.2% and 1.45% respectively, unless you’ve crossed the wage base.
  • Track year-to-date wages against the Social Security wage base to anticipate a jump in net pay.
  • For contractors, confirm no withholding was taken, since that would be unusual and worth a payer conversation.

Pro Tip: If your withholding consistently feels too high or too low relative to your tax bracket, update your Form W-4 rather than waiting until filing season to adjust.

Contractors who find estimated payments falling short should revise quarterly amounts using Form 1040-ES rather than waiting for the next deadline. Persistent classification or reporting errors that payroll won’t correct are worth escalating to a tax professional.

FAQ

How much do I get taxed on my commission?

As an employee, your employer typically withholds federal income tax at the flat 22% supplemental rate, plus 6.2% Social Security and 1.45% Medicare, per Publication 15. Your actual tax liability depends on your total annual income and bracket, so the amount withheld may differ from what you owe at filing.

Do I have to pay taxes if I do commissions?

Yes, commission income is taxable whether you’re an employee or an independent contractor. Employees have income tax and FICA withheld automatically, while contractors must pay income tax and the 15.3% self-employment tax through quarterly estimated payments, as outlined by the self-employed individuals tax center.

Are bonuses taxed at 40%?

The IRS allows employers to withhold a flat 22% on supplemental wages under most circumstances, rising to 37% only on amounts that push total supplemental wages above $1 million in a year, according to Publication 15.

How much is a $10,000 bonus after taxes?

This is withholding, not final liability, since your actual tax owed depends on your total income for the year.

What’s the difference between draw and commission for tax purposes?

A draw is an advance against future commissions and is typically taxed the same way regular wages or commissions are when paid, through standard or supplemental withholding. The tax treatment depends on your classification as an employee or contractor, not on whether the payment is labeled a draw or a commission.
