Is Per Diem Taxable? 2 Examples, Payroll Steps and Net Pay Calculator

Published September 28, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Per diem is generally not taxable to an employee when it is paid under an accountable plan and properly substantiated. The Publication 463 framework, together with the GSA per diem rates, sets the benchmarks that determine this outcome. Nontaxable treatment requires business connection, adequate accounting, and timely return of any excess. Common exceptions include amounts above the federal rate, missing substantiation, and assignments that stop qualifying as temporary.

TL;DR:

  • Reimbursing per diem at or below the federal rates keeps it non-taxable, provided the employee substantiates travel details and returns excess within 120 days.
  • Any per diem paid without proper documentation, above the federal rate, or not reimbursed promptly becomes taxable wages subject to payroll withholding.
  • Employer policies must set clear deadlines for substantiation within 60 days and return of excess within 120 days to maintain accountable plan status.
  • Special rules apply for indefinite assignments over one year, transportation industry workers, and water travel, which may alter tax treatment or documentation requirements.
  • Using payroll modeling tools helps quantify the pay impact when per diem exceeds federal rates and becomes taxable.

What per diem covers and how federal benchmark rates work

Per diem reimburses two categories of travel cost: lodging, and meals and incidental expenses, usually shortened to M&IE. Incidental expenses include items like fees for baggage handlers and hotel staff tips, not laundry or transportation between work sites. Employers can use the federal per diem rate as a ceiling for tax-free reimbursement, or set their own rate, but paying above the federal benchmark creates tax exposure on the difference.

The GSA per diem bulletin sets FY 2026 CONUS per diem rates effective October 1, 2025, covering the standard federal benchmarks for lodging and M&IE used by employers nationwide. Rates vary by locality, with higher-cost cities carrying separate lodging and meal ceilings than standard-rate locations.

FY 2026 CONUS per diem rates are published by the GSA, and they reset every fiscal year based on locality cost surveys, according to the GSA per diem bulletin. Employers that reimburse at these rates and satisfy the accountable-plan test avoid treating the payment as wages.

A few structural points matter for payroll accuracy:

  • Lodging and M&IE are tracked separately, and an employer may reimburse actual lodging costs while using a fixed M&IE rate.
  • The standard CONUS rate applies to most locations; specific cities and counties carry their own higher rates listed in the GSA tables.
  • Employers may adopt the high-low substantiation method, which uses just two rates nationwide instead of hundreds of locality figures.
  • Transportation workers, such as interstate truck drivers, qualify for a special M&IE rate under Publication 463.

Choosing a rate above the federal benchmark is legal, but the portion exceeding the GSA figure is treated as taxable wages unless the employee substantiates actual expenses that justify it.

The three-part accountable plan test employers must satisfy

The IRS accountable-plan test decides whether per diem stays out of an employee’s taxable wages. The IRS per diem FAQ explains that calling a payment “per diem” does not by itself exempt it from tax. The payment must meet three conditions.

  1. Business connection. The expense must be paid or incurred while performing services as an employee, for a legitimate business purpose such as travel away from the employee’s tax home.
  2. Adequate accounting. The employee must substantiate the amount, time, place, and business purpose of the expense within a reasonable period, generally understood as 60 days after the expense is paid or incurred.
  3. Return of excess. Any advance that exceeds substantiated expenses must be returned to the employer within a reasonable period, commonly cited as 120 days after the expense is paid or incurred.

Adequate accounting under the per diem substantiation method is lighter than requiring itemized receipts for every meal. Under Revenue Procedure RP-19-48, an employee using a per diem rate at or below the federal amount is deemed to have substantiated the expense amount simply by reporting the date, place, and business purpose of the trip. Receipts become necessary again when the employer pays a meals-only per diem but reimburses lodging separately based on actual cost, since lodging still needs documentary proof.

Pro Tip: Build the 120-day return-of-excess deadline directly into your expense report software so unreturned advances get flagged automatically instead of surfacing during a payroll audit.

If any one of the three elements fails, the entire per diem payment, not just the shortfall, can lose its accountable-plan protection. The per diem FAQ treats per diem as an administrative substantiation method rather than a label that automatically shields the payment from payroll tax. Payroll teams should treat every per diem advance as conditional until the accounting and return-of-excess steps are actually completed.

When per diem becomes taxable and what payroll must do

Per diem crosses into taxable wages under several common scenarios, and each one triggers a specific payroll response.

  • Nonaccountable plans. A flat travel allowance paid with no requirement to substantiate or return unused amounts is taxable from dollar one, regardless of the amount.
  • Excess not returned. When an employee receives more than substantiated expenses and does not return the excess within the employer’s reasonable period, the excess becomes wages.
  • Missing substantiation. Per diem paid without the required date, place, and business-purpose documentation loses its nontaxable status even if the rate itself was reasonable.
  • Day trips without an overnight stay. Per diem for meals on a trip that does not include an overnight absence from the tax home is generally taxable, since the travel-away-from-home requirement is not met.

Once a payment is determined to be taxable, payroll withholds federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare on the taxable portion, the same as on regular wages. The per diem FAQ recommends processing unreturned excess as wages in the payroll period following the end of the reasonable substantiation period, so withholding does not lag far behind the exposure.

On Form W-2, the substantiated, nontaxable portion of per diem is reported informationally in Box 12 using Code L. Any taxable excess or nonaccountable-plan payment instead gets added to Box 1 wages, and to Boxes 3 and 5 for Social Security and Medicare wages. Getting this split wrong is one of the most frequent per diem payroll errors, since it either overstates taxable income for compliant travelers or understates it for employees who never returned an advance.

Substantiation checklist for employees and employers

Substantiation is what keeps a per diem payment out of taxable wages, and the requirements are specific enough to build into a standard form.

An employee expense report should include:

  • Dates of travel, matched to the business trip’s actual start and end.
  • Business destination, city or locality, tied to the GSA rate used.
  • Business purpose, described specifically enough to show the travel connection.
  • Amount claimed, using the per diem rate rather than itemized receipts, unless lodging is reimbursed separately at actual cost.

Pro Tip: Keep lodging receipts even when using a meals-only per diem rate. The meal allowance is deemed substantiated by the per diem method, but lodging reimbursed at actual cost still needs documentary proof.

Employers should collect these reports within the same reasonable period that governs return of excess, generally 60 days for filing the accounting and 120 days for returning any unused advance, per the standard set out in Revenue Procedure RP-19-48. Retaining expense reports and per diem records for at least the standard payroll retention period protects both parties if a filing is questioned later. A consistent intake process, whether a paper form or a payroll system field, removes the ambiguity that turns a compliant per diem policy into a wage-reporting problem.

Employer payroll steps when per diem becomes taxable

When a portion of per diem fails the accountable-plan test, payroll has a defined sequence to follow rather than a judgment call.

  1. Calculate the taxable excess. Subtract the substantiated, federal-rate-or-below amount from the total per diem paid; the difference is the taxable figure.
  2. Time the withholding. Add the taxable excess to wages in the payroll period following the end of the substantiation window, consistent with the timing guidance in Revenue Procedure RP-19-48.
  3. Apply standard payroll taxes. Withhold federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare on the taxable portion exactly as on regular wages, since it is now treated the same way.
  4. Split the W-2 reporting. Report the substantiated, nontaxable per diem in Box 12 with Code L, and fold the taxable excess into Box 1, Box 3, and Box 5 wages.

IRS Publication 5137 includes worked reporting examples showing exactly this split between excluded per diem and taxable excess, which is a useful reference when a payroll system’s default template does not separate the two automatically.

Special rules: temporary assignments, transport workers, and water travel

Several exceptions change how the basic per diem rules apply, and missing one is a common source of misclassified travel pay.

  • The one-year rule. IRS Topic 511 explains that an assignment expected to last more than one year is not “temporary,” so travel-away-from-home treatment, including per diem, stops applying once that threshold is crossed or realistically expected.
  • Indefinite assignments. If an assignment’s realistic expected duration exceeds one year from the start, it is treated as indefinite from day one, not just after twelve months pass.
  • Transportation workers. Employees in the transportation industry, such as interstate truck and bus drivers, can use a special, separately published M&IE rate instead of standard locality rates, per Publication 463.
  • High-low method. Employers can simplify substantiation by using just two rates nationwide, one for designated high-cost localities and one for everywhere else, rather than tracking hundreds of individual GSA locality rates.
  • Luxury water travel. Per diem for travel by ocean liner, cruise ship, or other luxury water transportation carries its own daily dollar limits under Publication 463, and first and last travel days are typically reimbursed at a partial rate.

Worked examples: nontaxable per diem versus taxable excess

Two short examples show how the accountable-plan test plays out in dollars.

  1. Example A, compliant per diem. An employee travels for three days to a standard CONUS locality and receives the federal M&IE rate each day, substantiating dates, location, and business purpose on a timely expense report. Because the payment matches the federal benchmark and every accountable-plan element is met, the full amount is nontaxable and reported only informationally under Code L.
  2. Example B, excess not returned. Say an employer pays 120% of the federal M&IE rate for the same three-day trip and the employee never returns the excess 20%. That excess portion, the amount above the federal rate, becomes taxable wages: it gets added to Box 1, Box 3, and Box 5, and payroll withholds federal income tax, Social Security, and Medicare on it in the following pay period.

The per diem substantiation method deems federal-rate reimbursements adequately accounted for, according to the per diem FAQ, which is why Example A needs no receipts while Example B’s excess still triggers full payroll tax treatment. Running either scenario through a take-home pay calculator shows the withholding difference in real numbers rather than percentages, and generating a pay stub afterward keeps the employee’s own records aligned with payroll reporting.

Practical priorities for HR teams and business travelers

Getting per diem right comes down to a short list of habits, not a complex system.

  • Set a written per diem policy that names the rate source, the substantiation deadline, and the excess-return deadline in plain language.
  • Build the 60-day accounting window and 120-day return window into whatever expense software or form employees actually use.
  • Flag any per diem rate set above the federal benchmark for automatic review before it hits payroll.
  • Reconcile Code L amounts against taxable wage entries every pay cycle, not just at year end.
  • Employees should log date, place, and business purpose the same day as travel, keep lodging receipts even under a meals-only per diem, and return unused advances before the deadline rather than waiting to be asked.

FAQ

What are the IRS rules for per diem?

The IRS treats per diem as nontaxable only when paid under an accountable plan: the expense must have a business connection, the employee must adequately account for it within a reasonable period, and any excess advance must be returned, according to the IRS per diem FAQ. Payments that skip any of these three steps are treated as wages subject to withholding.

What are the disadvantages of per diem?

Per diem can create payroll complexity when an employer sets a rate above the federal benchmark, since the excess above the GSA rate becomes taxable unless separately justified. It also requires consistent tracking of substantiation deadlines and return-of-excess windows, and a single missed step can convert an otherwise compliant payment into wages.

What is the IRS per diem rate for meals in 2026?

The GSA sets M&IE rates by locality as part of its FY 2026 CONUS per diem bulletin, effective October 1, 2025, so the applicable meal rate depends on the specific travel destination rather than a single flat number. The current locality-by-locality figures are published in the GSA per diem bulletin.

What is the 300% rule for per diem?

Readers encountering that term should check the specific policy or source describing it, since it is not part of the standard federal per diem framework in Publication 463 or the GSA per diem bulletin.
