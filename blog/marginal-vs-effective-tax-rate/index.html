Marginal vs. Effective Tax Rates: 22% Isn’t Your Whole Bill

Published October 13, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Your marginal tax rate is the rate charged on your next dollar of taxable income, based on your statutory bracket. Your effective tax rate is your total tax liability divided by your total income, showing the overall share you actually pay. Use the marginal rate to evaluate a raise, bonus, or extra freelance income. Use the effective rate to understand your real annual tax burden.

TL;DR:

  • For 2026, a single filer’s 22% bracket begins above $50,400, but that rate applies only to income within the bracket; joint thresholds are double.
  • Before selling an investment near a bracket threshold, check whether the added income would move your last dollar into a higher bracket.
  • For an effective rate, dividing tax by taxable income yields a higher figure than using gross or adjusted gross income, because deductions shrink the denominator.
  • Payroll taxes and credit phaseouts can raise or lower the true marginal cost of extra income beyond the statutory bracket rate.
  • For retirees, additional income can make up to 85% of Social Security benefits taxable, depending on combined income, increasing its marginal tax burden.

Definitions and How Progressive Tax Brackets Work for 2026

The marginal tax rate is the statutory percentage applied to the last dollar you earn. The effective, or average, tax rate is total tax divided by total income, a figure the Tax Policy Center frames as a backward-looking snapshot of overall burden rather than a tool for forecasting decisions.

Federal income tax in the United States is progressive. Each bracket taxes only the income that falls inside it, not your entire income. A single filer with taxable income in the 22% bracket does not pay 22% on all of it; the lower portions are still taxed at lower rates.

For tax year 2026, the IRS federal brackets for single filers are:

  • 10% on income up to $12,400
  • 12% on income over $12,400
  • 22% on income over $50,400
  • 24% on income over $105,700
  • 32% on income over $201,775
  • 35% on income over $256,225
  • 37% on income over $640,600

Married filing jointly thresholds are exactly double these amounts. Our 2026 tax brackets breakdown walks through how these thresholds translate into take-home pay across filing statuses.

How to Compute Effective and Marginal Rates Step by Step

Effective tax rate is calculated as total tax liability divided by total income. The choice between using adjusted gross income or taxable income as the denominator changes the result: dividing by taxable income (after deductions) produces a higher effective rate than dividing by gross or adjusted gross income, since the denominator is smaller.

Your marginal rate is simpler to find. Locate your taxable income on the bracket table and the rate attached to the bracket your last dollar falls into is your statutory marginal rate.

A reproducible method:

  1. Determine your taxable income after deductions and exemptions.
  2. Calculate tax owed layer by layer, applying each bracket’s rate only to the income within that bracket, or reference a tax table for the same result.
  3. Divide total tax owed by total income to get your effective rate.
  4. Identify the bracket containing your last dollar of income: that rate is your marginal rate for next-dollar decisions.

Pro Tip: Add payroll taxes like Social Security and Medicare to your marginal calculation if you want the full marginal cost of an extra hour or bonus, not just the income tax portion.

Two Worked Examples Comparing Both Rates

Example A: A single filer with $100,000 in taxable income for 2026. Using the IRS brackets, tax is layered at 10% on the first $12,400, 12% on the next portion up to $50,400, 22% up to $100,000. The last dollar sits in the 22% bracket, so 22% is the marginal rate. Total tax divided by $100,000 lands well under 22%, since lower brackets covered most of the income at cheaper rates. That gap is why the effective rate always trails the marginal rate under a layered system.

  • A bonus that falls within the 22% bracket is taxed at the 22% marginal rate, not the lower effective rate.
  • Budgeting for annual tax owed should use the effective rate, not the marginal one.

Example B: A single filer with taxable income in the 32% bracket faces a higher marginal rate on each additional dollar, yet the effective rate still reflects the blended cost of every lower bracket beneath it. The higher the income, the wider that gap tends to grow, since more income gets taxed at the lower brackets layered underneath the top one.

Which Rate Answers Which Question

The right rate depends on the question you are asking, not on which one sounds more official.

  • Deciding whether to take overtime, a side gig, or a bonus: use your marginal rate, since it tells you what the next dollar actually costs.
  • Comparing your tax burden year over year or against another household: use your effective rate.
  • Timing the sale of an investment near a bracket threshold: check your marginal rate at that income level before deciding when to sell.
  • Negotiating a salary: your marginal rate shows what you keep from the raise itself; your effective rate shows what happens to your whole paycheck.

Pro Tip: If the question starts with “how much more will I keep,” reach for the marginal rate. If it starts with “what share of my income goes to taxes,” reach for the effective rate.

Payroll Taxes, Credits, and the Real Marginal Rate

Statutory brackets do not capture the full incremental cost of an extra dollar. Tax Foundation analysis notes that effective marginal tax rates combine income tax with payroll taxes and credit phaseouts, which can push the real rate well above or below the bracket rate.

  • Social Security and Medicare taxes add to the marginal cost of earned income, though the Social Security portion stops once wages exceed the annual wage base cap.
  • Credits like the Earned Income Tax Credit and Child Tax Credit phase in and out with income, which can create negative marginal rates during phase-in and sharply elevated marginal rates during phaseout.
  • Extra non-Social-Security income can make a larger share of Social Security benefits taxable. SSA guidance notes up to 85% of benefits may become taxable depending on combined income, which raises the effective marginal burden on that additional income for retirees.

How I Recommend You Use Both Numbers

I recommend computing both rates before any major income decision: negotiating pay, taking on overtime, or realizing a capital gain. The marginal rate tells you the immediate cost; the effective rate tells you the bigger picture once the year closes out.

FAQ

What does your marginal tax rate equal?

Your marginal tax rate equals the statutory rate applied to your last dollar of taxable income, found by matching your taxable income to the current bracket table. For 2026, single filers’ top bracket reaches 37% on income over $640,600.

What states let you keep all of your Social Security and 401(k) income?

State tax treatment of Social Security benefits and 401(k) withdrawals varies widely, and several states exempt one or both from state income tax while others do not. Because rules differ by state and change periodically, check your specific state’s current tax code or a state-by-state guide before assuming an exemption applies to you.

What’s the difference between marginal rate and effective rate?

The marginal rate is the tax charged on your next dollar of income, while the effective rate is your total tax divided by your total income. The Tax Policy Center notes the effective rate is always lower than the marginal rate under a progressive system, since earlier income is taxed at lower brackets.

What is the difference between property tax rate and effective property tax rate?

A property tax rate is typically the statutory rate set by a local taxing authority and applied to assessed value, while an effective property tax rate reflects the actual tax paid as a percentage of a property’s market value. The two can differ because of assessment ratios, exemptions, or caps that vary by jurisdiction.
