2026 U.S. Tax Brackets: 7 IRS Rates and Your Take Home Pay

Published September 24, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

For 2026, the U.S. ordinary individual income tax system uses seven marginal rates: 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37%. Every threshold moved up for inflation, so the income ranges attached to each rate are wider than they were for 2025. Only the portion of income inside a given bracket gets taxed at that bracket’s rate. Check the tables below to find where your income lands.

TL;DR:

  • The top income tax rate of 37% applies only to earnings above $640,600 for single filers and $768,700 for married couples filing jointly in 2026.
  • Most filers’ effective tax rates are significantly lower than their marginal rates, often around 10-15% for middle-income earners due to progressive brackets.
  • High earners subject to the net investment income tax and alternative minimum tax must account for additional surtaxes that are not reflected in the ordinary brackets.
  • State income taxes vary widely, with nine states not taxing income at all, which can substantially impact net pay compared to federal estimates.
  • Proper paycheck planning requires considering pre-tax deductions, credits, state taxes, and income sources rather than relying solely on bracket thresholds.

2026 Federal Income Tax Brackets by Filing Status

The IRS locked in these thresholds through Rev. Proc. 2025-32, which sets the official rate tables and the per-filing-status formulas used to calculate tax across layered brackets. The IRS newsroom release confirms the same cutoffs for the seven ordinary rates.

Married filing jointly

Head of household brackets sit between the single and joint schedules, while married filing separately mirrors the single brackets at roughly half the dollar thresholds for the upper rates. Estates and trusts follow a much steeper compressed schedule, reaching the 37% rate at a small fraction of the income levels that apply to individuals, per the Rev. Proc. 2025-32 tables.

The top marginal threshold lands at $640,600 for single filers and $768,700 for married couples filing jointly. Anyone earning above those marks pays 37% only on the slice of income exceeding the threshold, not on their entire earnings.

Marginal Rate vs. Effective Tax Rate: A Step-by-Step Example

A marginal rate is the tax rate applied to your last dollar of taxable income. Your effective rate is the average rate you actually pay once every bracket layer is added up and divided by your income.

Here’s how a single filer with taxable income would work through the 2026 brackets:

  1. The first $12,400 is taxed at 10%, producing $1,240.
  2. The next portion, from $12,400 to $50,400, is taxed at 12%.
  3. The remaining taxable income above $50,400 is taxed at 22%.
  4. Total federal tax due is the sum of taxes across all bracket layers.
  5. Divide total tax by taxable income to find the effective tax rate.

This filer’s effective tax rate is less than their top marginal rate of 22%. That gap between the marginal rate and the effective rate is the single most misunderstood piece of the whole bracket system. A lot of people assume their entire income gets taxed at their top rate, and that mistake alone leads to some very wrong paycheck estimates.

2026 Standard Deduction and What Lowers Your Taxable Income

Nobody pays tax on gross income. The brackets apply to taxable income, which starts with gross pay and subtracts deductions. The 2026 standard deduction amounts are:

  • Single: $16,100
  • Married filing jointly: $32,200
  • Head of household: $24,150
  • Married filing separately: $16,100

Pre-tax contributions shrink taxable income even further before the standard deduction ever comes into play. Money routed into a traditional 401(k) or a Health Savings Account never shows up as taxable wages in the first place, which pulls income out of your top bracket layer by layer.

Deciding whether to itemize instead of taking the standard deduction comes down to one comparison: add up mortgage interest, state and local taxes (capped at $10,000), and charitable gifts. If that total beats your standard deduction amount, itemizing wins. For most filers in 2026, given the higher standard deduction figures, itemizing only makes sense with a mortgage or heavy charitable giving.

Capital Gains, AMT, and NIIT: Rates the Brackets Don’t Cover

Long-term capital gains and qualified dividends run on a separate schedule from ordinary wages, and mixing the two regimes is one of the most common tax-planning errors. The Tax Foundation’s 2026 summary confirms these thresholds sit apart from the seven ordinary rates.

Filing status 0% rate below 15% rate up to 20% for income above
Single $44,625 $553,850 $553,850
Married filing jointly $89,250 $647,850 $647,850

Two other regimes can override your ordinary bracket math entirely:

  • The alternative minimum tax (AMT) runs at 26% or 28%, and it only kicks in for filers with certain high-value deductions or exclusions that trigger the parallel calculation.
  • The net investment income tax (NIIT) adds 3.8% on investment income once modified adjusted gross income crosses set thresholds, layered on top of whatever capital gains rate already applies.

Treat ordinary-income planning and capital gains planning as two separate exercises. Applying your ordinary marginal rate to investment gains, or vice versa, will throw off any liability estimate.

Why a Bracket Lookup Isn’t Enough for Paycheck Planning

Knowing your bracket tells you your tax liability at the end of the year. It doesn’t tell you what your employer should withhold from each paycheck. IRS Publication 505 lays out the additional variables that determine accurate withholding.

Run through this checklist before you assume your paycheck reflects your real tax situation:

  • Expected gross income for the year, including bonuses and overtime.
  • Pre-tax deductions such as 401(k) contributions, HSA funding, or health premiums.
  • Credits you expect to claim, including the child tax credit or education credits.
  • Other income sources: freelance work, investment income, a second job.
  • Pay frequency, since weekly, biweekly, and monthly schedules all spread withholding differently across the year.

Filing status alone doesn’t capture any of that nuance. Two single filers earning the same salary can owe very different amounts in April if one has a side gig and the other doesn’t.

What Changed for 2026: Inflation Adjustments and Legislative Notes

The 2026 tables blend two forces: routine inflation indexing and adjustments tied to the One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act, according to the IRS newsroom release. A few changes worth flagging:

  • Standard deduction amounts rose across every filing status, following the inflation-adjustment schedule.
  • The 401(k) contribution limit increased to $24,500 for 2026, and the IRA limit rose to $7,500.
  • Certain refundable credit rules were adjusted under the legislative amendments included in the release.

Rev. Proc. 2025-32 itself warns that legislation enacted after its October 9, 2025 cutoff could still change these figures. If you’re filing after any late-year tax legislation, cross-check the current IRS release before finalizing your numbers rather than relying on any single snapshot of the tables.

Two Worked Examples: Single and Married Filing Jointly

Example 1: Single filer, $95,000 gross income

  1. Subtract the $16,100 standard deduction: taxable income is $78,900.
  2. Apply the layers: 10% on the first $12,400 ($1,240), 12% on the next $38,000 ($4,560), and 22% on the remaining $28,500 ($6,270).
  3. Total tax: roughly $12,070.
  4. Effective rate: $12,070 ÷ $95,000, about 12.7%.

Example 2: Married filing jointly, $150,000 combined gross income

  1. Subtract the $32,200 standard deduction: taxable income is $117,800.
  2. Apply the layers: 10% on the first $24,800 ($2,480), 12% on the next $76,000 ($9,120), and 22% on the remaining $17,000 ($3,740).
  3. Total tax: roughly $15,340.
  4. Effective rate: $15,340 ÷ $150,000, about 10.2%.

Both examples use the exact 2026 thresholds from Rev. Proc. 2025-32. Notice the joint filer’s effective rate lands lower despite higher gross income, a direct result of wider bracket thresholds for married couples.

How State Income Tax Changes Your Real Tax Burden

Federal brackets only tell half the story. Where you live determines whether a second layer of tax gets added on top of the federal numbers above.

Nine states, including Texas, Florida, and Washington, charge no state income tax at all, so residents there keep the full benefit of whatever the federal brackets leave them. A handful of others, like New Hampshire, only tax specific investment income rather than wages.

Most states run their own graduated brackets, separate from the federal system, with their own standard deductions and thresholds. A few use a single flat rate applied to all taxable income regardless of bracket. California’s top marginal state rate is among the highest in the country, while a state like Colorado applies one flat percentage to everyone.

The practical effect: two people earning identical federal taxable income can end up with meaningfully different take-home pay depending on their state’s structure. That’s the biggest blind spot in any federal-only bracket estimate. Someone comparing job offers in different states needs both the federal table and the destination state’s rules to get an accurate net-pay picture, since a lower gross salary in a no-tax state can beat a higher salary in a high-tax one.

Phase-Outs and Limits That Kick In at Higher Incomes

Several tax benefits shrink or disappear entirely as income rises, independent of which marginal bracket you’re in. These phase-outs catch a lot of filers off guard because they don’t show up in a simple bracket lookup.

The child tax credit begins phasing out once modified adjusted gross income crosses set thresholds, reducing the credit gradually rather than cutting it off all at once. Contribution eligibility for a Roth IRA phases out entirely above certain income levels, pushing higher earners toward backdoor conversion strategies instead. The deduction for traditional IRA contributions phases out too, but only when the filer or their spouse is covered by a workplace retirement plan.

Above the top capital gains thresholds detailed earlier, the NIIT surtax adds another 3.8% layer on investment income.

None of these phase-outs move in lockstep with the seven ordinary brackets. Building a full 2026 tax estimate means checking both the bracket table and any phase-out thresholds tied to specific credits or deductions you plan to claim.

The Gap Between Knowing Your Bracket and Knowing Your Tax Bill

Most people who search for tax bracket tables want reassurance more than data. They want to know whether a raise will “push them into a higher bracket” and cost them money overall. It won’t, and that misunderstanding is worth addressing directly rather than glossing over.

The bigger blind spot isn’t the ordinary brackets themselves. It’s how many people skip straight from “here’s my bracket” to “here’s my paycheck” without accounting for pre-tax deductions, phase-outs, state tax structure, or the difference between marginal and effective rates. A bracket table answers one question. A paycheck involves at least five.

FAQ

What are the tax brackets for 2026?

The 37% top rate starts above $640,600 for single filers and $768,700 for married couples filing jointly.

What states let you keep all of your Social Security and 401(k) income?

States with no income tax, including Texas, Florida, Nevada, and Washington, don’t tax Social Security benefits or 401(k) withdrawals at the state level. Several other states that do levy income tax still specifically exempt Social Security benefits, so the rules vary and are worth checking against your specific state before assuming either way.

Who gets the new tax break tied to recent legislation?

Certain provisions tied to the One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act adjusted specific deductions and credits for 2026, as outlined in the IRS’s 2026 inflation adjustment release. Eligibility depends on your specific filing status and income level, so check the release or a calculator like Paysums’ income tax calculator against your own numbers.

How do I calculate my 2026 taxes using the brackets?

Subtract your standard deduction (or itemized total) from your gross income to get taxable income, then apply each bracket’s rate to the portion of income that falls inside it. Add the layers together for total tax owed, then divide by your income to find your effective rate, or use the take-home pay calculator to run the full calculation automatically.

Is my capital gains rate the same as my ordinary income tax bracket?

No. Proc. 2025-32. Applying your ordinary marginal rate to investment gains will overstate or understate your actual liability.
