Hourly vs. Salary in the U.S.: Which Offer Pays More After Taxes?

Published October 10, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Salaried pay gives you a predictable paycheck and often broader benefits, while hourly pay ties your earnings directly to the hours you work, including overtime potential that salaried roles usually do not offer. The practical next step is to convert both offers to the same basis, typically an annual total, and compare total compensation and take-home pay rather than the headline number alone.

TL;DR:

  • A salaried title does not decide overtime eligibility; most exemptions require a duties test and at least $684 weekly, with special rules for some industries.
  • $30 hourly pay reaches $62,400 at 40 hours weekly, but five overtime hours each week add about $11,700, bringing annual earnings near $74,100.
  • BLS data put civilian benefits at $15 per hour worked, versus $32.92 in wages; add employer coverage, leave, and retirement matching when valuing offers.
  • Before accepting hourly work, ask for guaranteed weekly hours, then compare guaranteed, typical, and peak earnings alongside benefits and estimated take home pay.

What salary and hourly pay mean in practice

Salaried pay is a fixed annual amount divided across pay periods, regardless of how many hours a given week actually requires. Employers typically present it as an annual figure, then split it into weekly, biweekly, or monthly paychecks that stay the same even when a project runs long or a week is short.

Hourly pay is calculated from a wage rate multiplied by hours actually worked, so pay varies with the schedule. Timekeeping, usually a time clock or digital tracker, records hours and separates regular time from overtime, and paychecks reflect exactly what was logged during that pay period.

The terms “salaried” and “hourly” describe how pay is calculated, while “exempt” and “nonexempt” describe overtime eligibility under federal law. The two labels often overlap but are not identical:

  • Most hourly workers are nonexempt and qualify for overtime pay.
  • Most salaried workers in professional, administrative, or executive roles are exempt from overtime.
  • A worker can be salaried and still nonexempt, which means overtime rules still apply despite the fixed paycheck.
  • A worker is rarely hourly and exempt, since exemption generally requires a predetermined salary basis.

Knowing which category you fall into determines whether extra hours translate into extra pay, which is the central question most people weighing a salaried offer against an hourly one are actually trying to answer.

Overtime and exempt vs nonexempt: how the law changes pay outcomes

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) requires nonexempt employees to receive overtime pay, time and a half their regular rate, for hours worked beyond 40 in a workweek. Exempt employees, by contrast, receive no additional pay no matter how many hours a role demands in a given week.

The Department of Labor sets the salary-level threshold for most executive, administrative, and professional exemptions at $684 per week, or $35,568 annually, with a separate $107,432 annual threshold for highly compensated employees. A salaried employee paid below that threshold typically cannot be classified as exempt, regardless of job title, and must receive overtime pay like an hourly worker.

Classification also depends on a duties test, not salary alone. An employee’s primary responsibilities, whether they involve independent judgment, management, or specialized professional work, factor into whether the exemption legitimately applies. The Department of Labor’s fact sheet on overtime rules walks through how the salary-basis and duties tests interact, and misclassifying a worker as exempt when the duties test fails can mean back pay owed for unpaid overtime.

The interaction between salaried status and additional hourly work is more nuanced than a simple either-or. A Department of Labor opinion letter addressing dual-role pay confirms that an employee paid a salary for an exempt position can take on additional nonexempt hourly work without automatically losing exempt status, provided the primary duty and salary basis tests still hold. This matters for anyone evaluating a hybrid offer, since extra hourly compensation on top of a salary is not necessarily a red flag for misclassification.

Special salary levels also apply in certain US territories and industries, so the standard threshold does not universally apply to every worker nationwide. Checking your specific classification against the current DOL threshold is the only reliable way to know whether overtime should appear on your paycheck.

How to convert salary to hourly with worked examples

Comparing a salaried offer to an hourly one starts with putting both numbers on the same footing. Two formulas cover most cases:

  1. Salary to hourly: divide the annual salary by expected paid hours per year, commonly 2,080 hours for a standard 40 hour week.
  2. Hourly to salary: multiply the hourly rate by expected annual hours, then add any anticipated overtime pay separately.

A $40,000 annual salary divided by 2,080 hours works out to roughly $19.23 an hour, assuming a standard full-time schedule with no unpaid leave. A $30 hourly rate at 2,080 hours annualizes to $62,400 before overtime, but that figure only holds if the schedule stays at 40 hours a week year-round.

Overtime changes the math meaningfully. If that same $30 an hour worker regularly logs five hours of overtime weekly at time and a half, the overtime premium adds roughly $11,700 a year, pushing total annual earnings closer to $74,100. A salaried employee in a comparable role earning $62,400 would not see that bump regardless of extra hours worked, since the fixed salary absorbs any overtime.

Pay frequency affects per-period math but not the annual total. A biweekly salaried paycheck of $40,000 divides into 26 periods at roughly $1,538 each, while a monthly schedule divides into 12 periods at about $3,333 each, both representing the same yearly figure.

Modeling variable hours requires more care. Hourly workers with fluctuating schedules should calculate a realistic average workweek rather than assuming a flat 40 hours, and should separate a “typical week” estimate from a “peak season” estimate that includes overtime. Our guide to calculating overtime pay walks through these formulas with additional worked examples for irregular schedules.

Benefits and total compensation: valuing employer-paid benefits

Base pay is only part of the comparison. Employer-paid benefits often represent a sizable share of total compensation, and ignoring them skews the decision toward whichever offer has the higher sticker number.

Benefits worth quantifying before comparing two offers include:

  • Health insurance premiums the employer covers.
  • Retirement plan matching, such as a 401(k) match percentage.
  • Paid time off, including vacation, sick leave, and holidays.
  • Supplemental pay like bonuses, shift differentials, or commissions.
  • Additional insurance, such as life or disability coverage.

Bureau of Labor Statistics data show employer costs for civilian worker compensation averaged about $47.92 per hour worked, with wages and salaries accounting for roughly $32.92 of that and benefits making up about $15.00 per hour. That split illustrates how benefits commonly add nearly a third of total compensation on top of base wages, which is exactly the portion a bare salary-versus-hourly comparison tends to miss.

A simple method for quantifying this: estimate the annual or hourly dollar value of your benefits package, often available from HR as an employer-cost percentage, and add it to your base pay. A salaried offer with strong health coverage and a generous retirement match can outpace a higher hourly rate once that full package is counted, and the reverse holds when an hourly role pays overtime consistently but offers thin benefits.

Taxes, withholding, and take-home pay differences

Your tax liability does not change based on whether you are paid a salary or an hourly wage. What does change is how withholding is calculated and timed, since payroll systems apply different formulas depending on pay frequency and whether income is regular or supplemental.

A few mechanics worth understanding when comparing offers or switching pay types:

  • Overtime pay and bonuses are often treated as supplemental wages, which can be withheld at a different rate than regular pay.
  • Switching from hourly to salaried, or the reverse, can shift your effective withholding if your Form W-4 is not updated to reflect the new income pattern.
  • Tipped income carries its own reporting rules that affect how much is withheld relative to take-home pay.
  • Pay frequency itself, weekly versus biweekly versus monthly, changes per-period withholding amounts even when annual tax owed stays the same.

IRS Publication 505 explains how employers use Form W-4 information alongside payroll period details to calculate withholding, which is why the same annual income can produce different per-paycheck withholding depending on how it’s structured. The IRS updated its Tax Withholding Estimator to account for recent legislative changes affecting supplemental wages, and recommends running the tool whenever your income structure changes. Doing this after accepting a new offer, whether moving to salary or to hourly work, helps avoid an unexpected tax bill or an overly thin paycheck throughout the year.

Practical checklist: exact numbers to collect and the questions to ask employers

Comparing offers accurately means gathering specific numbers before you negotiate or accept, not after. Collect these details from any employer making an offer:

  1. Base pay, stated as both the hourly rate and the annualized equivalent.
  2. Expected weekly or annual hours, including whether any minimum is guaranteed.
  3. Overtime policy, including the hourly rate paid for hours beyond 40 in a week.
  4. Paid time off allowance, counted in days or hours per year.
  5. Benefits details, including health insurance premiums and coverage tiers.
  6. Retirement plan match percentage and vesting schedule.
  7. Bonus or commission structure, including how often it’s paid and under what conditions.
  8. Pay frequency, since it affects budgeting even when the annual total is identical.

Once you have these numbers, compute three scenarios: a base scenario using guaranteed hours only, a typical scenario including realistic overtime or bonuses, and a full-year scenario adding any annual bonus or profit-sharing payout. This reveals how much the offers actually differ once variability is accounted for, rather than relying on a single headline figure.

Pro Tip: When negotiating an hourly offer, ask specifically for guaranteed minimum hours per week, since a high hourly rate with unpredictable scheduling can produce lower annual earnings than a moderate salary.

For benefits, ask about vesting timelines on retirement matches and waiting periods on health coverage, since a benefit that takes a year to vest changes its near-term value considerably.

When I usually recommend salaried versus hourly work

Salaried roles tend to fit people who value predictable paychecks, want benefits like retirement matching and paid leave bundled in, and are building toward a career track where exempt status is standard. Hourly roles tend to fit people who want overtime potential, shift differentials, or scheduling flexibility, particularly when the base rate is competitive and extra hours are realistically available.

Remote and flexible work arrangements can blur these lines further, since pay structures vary widely across industries and roles. FAQ

What Is a $40,000 Salary in Hourly Terms?

A $40,000 annual salary works out to about $19.23 an hour, assuming a standard 2,080 hour work year with no unpaid time off. This figure drops if you take unpaid leave and does not include the value of any employer-paid benefits.

What Does $30 an Hour Come Out to Annually?

At a standard 40 hour week with no overtime, $30 an hour annualizes to $62,400 before taxes. If regular overtime is part of the schedule, the annual total rises further since overtime hours are paid at time and a half under the FLSA.

What Is the Definition of Salary?

Salary is a fixed amount of pay an employee receives for a defined period, typically a year, divided across regular pay periods regardless of hours worked in any given week. It contrasts with hourly pay, which is calculated from a wage rate multiplied by hours actually logged.

How Does Salary Pay Work in Practice?

Salaried pay is split into equal paychecks across pay periods, such as weekly, biweekly, or monthly, based on the annual total agreed upon. Whether an employee is exempt from overtime depends on meeting the Department of Labor’s salary threshold and a duties test, not on the salaried label alone.

Can Overtime Pay Change a Salaried Offer’s True Value?

Most exempt salaried employees do not receive overtime pay regardless of hours worked, which caps earnings at the stated salary. Nonexempt salaried employees, by contrast, remain eligible for overtime under the same rules that apply to hourly workers.
