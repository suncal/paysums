Calculate Your 2026 Take Home, Gross vs Net Pay: Do You Keep 65–80%?

Budget with net pay, since that’s the money you can actually spend. Use gross pay for loan applications, salary negotiations, and anywhere someone asks for your “income.”

TL;DR:

Gross Pay vs Net Pay: The Definitions That Matter

Gross pay is the top-line figure on your pay stub, the total your employer owes you before anything is withheld. It includes your base salary or hourly wages, overtime, bonuses, commissions, and tips. The IRS is direct about this: all wages, salaries, and tips you receive for performing services as an employee count toward gross income, and your employer reports the total on Form W-2.

Net pay is the number that shows up as “Net Pay” or “Amount Paid” near the bottom of the stub, after every deduction has been subtracted.

  • Gross pay: total earnings, including overtime, bonuses, and tips, before withholding
  • Net pay: the deposited amount after taxes, benefits, and other deductions
  • YTD (year to date): a running total of gross pay, deductions, or net pay from January 1 through the current pay date, useful for spotting mid-year errors
  • Net income vs net pay: businesses use “net income” for profit after expenses; individuals use “net pay” for take-home wages. Don’t confuse the two terms when reading financial documents.

How Do You Calculate Gross and Net Pay?

Getting from gross to net follows a predictable sequence, whether you’re paid hourly or salaried.

  1. Calculate gross pay for hourly workers: multiply hours worked by your hourly rate, then add overtime hours multiplied by 1.5 times your rate.
  2. Calculate gross pay for salaried workers: divide annual salary by the number of pay periods (52 for weekly, 26 for biweekly, 24 for semimonthly, 12 for monthly).
  3. Gather withholding inputs: your W-4 filing status, pay frequency, state of residence, and any local tax jurisdiction.
  4. Apply pre-tax deductions first: traditional 401(k) contributions, HSA deposits, and employer health premiums reduce your taxable wages before taxes are calculated.
  5. Calculate federal withholding: the IRS uses progressive tax brackets applied to the wages remaining after pre-tax deductions, based on the W-4 you filed.
  6. Calculate FICA taxes: Social Security and Medicare are calculated on taxable wages, which may differ slightly from federal taxable wages depending on which benefits are pre-tax.
  7. Calculate state and local withholding, if applicable, using the same wage base logic.
  8. Subtract post-tax deductions: Roth contributions, wage garnishments, and union dues come out after taxes are already calculated.

The distinction between pre-tax and post-tax matters because it changes the base taxes are calculated on, not just the final total. A pre-tax 401(k) contribution shrinks your taxable wages, which lowers your federal withholding bill directly. A Roth contribution does not.

What Deductions Turn Gross Pay Into Net Pay?

Every paycheck runs through the same layers of subtraction, though the amounts vary by state, income, and elections.

  • Social Security: 6.2% of wages up to the annual wage base cap
  • Medicare: 1.45%, with an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax kicking in above certain income thresholds
  • Federal income tax: withheld using progressive brackets based on your W-4
  • State and local income tax: varies widely; a handful of states, including Texas and Florida, charge no state income tax at all
  • Pre-tax benefits: traditional 401(k), HSA contributions, and employer-sponsored health premiums
  • Post-tax items: Roth 401(k) contributions, wage garnishments, and union dues

Pro Tip: Federal effective tax rates rise sharply with income, according to Tax Foundation data, which is a big reason why two people with the same gross salary in different states or income brackets can see very different net pay.

Two Worked Examples: Gross to Net, Step by Step

Numbers make this concrete faster than any explanation. Two example workers, two different pay structures, same basic math.

Example A: a salaried employee earning $60,000 a year, paid biweekly (26 pay periods).

Line item Amount
Gross pay per period $2,307.69
Social Security (6.2%) $143.08
Medicare (1.45%) $33.46
Federal withholding (estimated) $210.00
State withholding (estimated) $85.00
Net pay $1,836.15
Percent retained About 80%

Example B: an hourly worker at $22/hour, 40 hours a week, with a 5% pre-tax 401(k) election.

  1. Gross weekly pay: 40 hours × $22 = $880.00
  2. Pre-tax 401(k) deduction: 5% of $880 = $44.00, leaving $836.00 in taxable wages
  3. Social Security and Medicare apply to the full $880, since 401(k) deferrals don’t reduce FICA wages
  4. Federal and state withholding apply to the reduced $836.00 taxable base
  5. Net pay lands in the high $600 range, depending on state tax and filing status

Order matters here. If that same worker hits the annual Social Security wage base cap mid-year, the 6.2% deduction disappears from every remaining paycheck, and net pay jumps noticeably for the rest of the year with no raise involved.

When Should You Use Gross Pay Instead of Net?

The right number depends entirely on what you’re doing with it. Mixing them up is one of the most common budgeting mistakes people make.

  • Use net pay for your monthly budget, rent affordability calculations, and emergency-fund targets, since that’s the actual cash available to spend
  • Use gross pay when a lender, landlord, or mortgage underwriter asks for your income, and when negotiating a salary offer, since employers and financial institutions think in gross terms
  • Understand the tradeoff: increasing a pre-tax 401(k) election lowers your net pay today in exchange for tax-deferred retirement savings, so a “smaller” paycheck isn’t necessarily a worse outcome

How to Check Your Pay Stub for Errors

A pay stub is only useful if the numbers on it are actually correct, and mistakes happen more often than people assume.

  1. Recalculate your gross pay against your hours worked or your salary divided by pay periods.
  2. Confirm Social Security is withheld at 6.2% and Medicare at 1.45%, and check whether you’ve crossed the annual wage base cap.
  3. Compare federal and state withholding against what your W-4 and state filing status should produce.
  4. Verify YTD totals on the current stub match the sum of prior stubs.
  5. Gather your most recent stub, your W-4, and your offer letter or employment contract before contacting HR.
  6. Ask payroll specific line-item questions rather than a general “does this look right,” since specific questions get faster, more accurate answers.

Pro Tip: Save one pay stub every time you change your W-4, adjust a 401(k) election, or get a raise. Comparing before-and-after stubs is the fastest way to confirm a change actually took effect correctly.

Why Up-to-Date Tax Tables Actually Change Your Numbers

An accurate take-home estimate depends on more inputs than most people expect: pay frequency, filing status, state, local tax jurisdiction, pre-tax elections, and any additional withholding you’ve requested. Miss one, and the estimate drifts.

Tax tables shift year to year, and 2026 brought a fresh round of rate updates across federal brackets and several state tables. A calculator running last year’s numbers will quietly misstate your net pay, sometimes by enough to matter for a monthly budget.

A Note on Keeping Paycheck Surprises Small

Review your W-4 after any major life event, marriage, a new job, a second income, and at least once a year regardless. Read your pay stub line by line after open enrollment or a raise, not just the bottom number. Build your budget on net pay, then double check it against one real pay stub whenever something changes.

FAQ

What Is the Main Difference Between Gross and Net Pay?

Gross pay is your total earnings before any deductions; net pay is what you actually receive after taxes and other withholdings come out. The IRS defines gross pay as the amount earned and net pay as the amount received after deductions like taxes are taken out.

What Does YTD Mean on a Pay Stub?

YTD stands for “year to date” and shows a running total of gross pay, deductions, or net pay from January 1 through the current pay period. It’s the fastest way to catch a payroll error, since a wrong YTD figure usually means a mistake happened on an earlier stub, not just this one.

What Percentage of Gross Pay Becomes Net Pay?

Most workers keep somewhere in the range of 65% to 80% of their gross pay as net pay, with the rest going to federal, state, and FICA taxes plus any elected deductions. The exact percentage depends heavily on your state, income level, and benefit elections, since federal effective tax rates vary significantly by income bracket.

What’s the Difference Between Exempt and Nonexempt Employees?

Exempt employees are paid a salary and are not entitled to overtime pay under federal wage law, while nonexempt employees, usually paid hourly, must receive overtime at 1.5 times their regular rate for hours worked beyond 40 in a week. Some salaried employees are still nonexempt depending on their duties and pay level, so the label depends on job function, not just pay structure.
