Freelancers: Pay Quarterly Estimated Taxes in 20 Minutes

Published September 21, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

If you expect to owe tax for 2026 after withholding and credits, the IRS generally requires quarterly estimated payments, due April, June, September, and January. Right now, the fastest safe move is to pick a method: match all of last year’s total tax (a higher percentage if your prior-year adjusted gross income was high), or calculate this year’s actual liability using Form 1040-ES, then send the first payment before the deadline.

TL;DR:

  • Most self-employed individuals expecting to owe over $1,000 after credits should make quarterly estimated tax payments, especially freelancers, gig workers, landlords, and investors.
  • The four quarterly deadlines for 2026 are April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15, with exact dates shifting if they fall on weekends or holidays.
  • Paying 100% of last year’s tax liability (or 110% if income was high) provides a penalty-proof safe harbor, while estimating 90% of this year’s projected tax requires diligent calculation.
  • Overpaying a quarter carries no penalty, but underpaying accrues interest and penalties calculated separately for each missed period.
  • Using IRS Direct Pay or EFTPS allows quick, record-logged payments, and maintaining confirmation records safeguards against disputes.

Who Has To Pay Quarterly Estimated Taxes?

The IRS threshold is simple on paper: if you expect to owe at least $1,000 in tax for the year after subtracting withholding and refundable credits, you’re on the hook for estimated payments. In practice, that catches a lot of people who don’t think of themselves as “business owners.”

  • 1099 contractors and freelancers with no employer withholding at all
  • Gig workers stacking multiple platforms without any tax withheld
  • Landlords or investors whose rental income and dividends outpace their W-2 withholding
  • Anyone who quit a job mid-year and lost their paycheck tax cushion

If you also hold a W-2 job, you can sometimes skip estimated payments entirely by asking that employer to withhold extra from each check instead. To determine if you need estimated payments, consider if you owed significant tax previously, have income with no withholding, or expect side income growth.

When Are the Quarterly Tax Deadlines?

The IRS splits the year into four payment periods, but they are not equal three-month chunks. The first period covers January through March, while the fourth covers only September through December, three months, not four, which trips up a lot of first-year filers.

Payment period Income earned Due date
Q1 January through March April 15, 2026
Q2 April through May June 15, 2026
Q3 June through August September 15, 2026
Q4 September through December January 15, 2027

When a due date falls on a weekend or federal holiday, it shifts to the next business day, so always confirm the exact date at IRS.gov before you pay. Most states with an income tax run on this same payment periods schedule, but a handful set their own dates, which the state section below covers.

How Do You Calculate Estimated Tax Payments?

You have two legitimate paths to a number, and they serve different situations.

  1. Safe harbor: Pay 100% of last year’s total tax liability (110% if your prior-year AGI exceeded $150,000, or $75,000 married filing separately), split into four payments. This locks in a penalty-proof number regardless of how this year turns out.
  2. Current-year estimate: Project this year’s income, subtract deductions, calculate the tax owed, and pay 90% of that total across four installments. This method saves cash if your income is dropping, but it demands more diligence.

Either way, self-employment tax has to enter the math separately from income tax. The Form 1040-ES worksheet instructs you to take 92.35% of your net profit as the base for SE tax, reflecting the portion an employer would otherwise cover, then apply the SE tax rate to that base. Half of the resulting SE tax is deductible when you estimate your adjusted gross income on the income tax side of the calculation.

Pro Tip: Overpaying an estimated quarter carries no penalty at all. Underpaying does. When your numbers are fuzzy, round up.

Here’s a simplified walkthrough for a freelance designer projecting $80,000 in net profit for the year:

  1. Multiply $80,000 by 92.35% to find the SE tax base.
  2. Apply the self-employment tax rate to that base to get total SE tax owed for the year.
  3. Deduct half of that SE tax amount from gross income to estimate AGI.
  4. Apply the standard deduction and calculate income tax on the resulting taxable income.
  5. Add income tax and SE tax together, then divide by four for each quarterly payment, adjusting for any withholding already covered elsewhere.

Re-run this estimate whenever a client pays a large invoice late, a contract ends early, or you land unexpected work. A 1099 Tax Calculator built specifically for self-employment tax and quarterly figures speeds up this recalculation without redoing the worksheet by hand each time.

What Are Your Options for Paying Estimated Taxes?

Once you know the number, picking a payment channel is the easy part. Each option trades speed, fees, and paperwork differently.

  • IRS Direct Pay: Free, processes from a bank account, and gives you an immediate confirmation number. This is the default choice for most individual filers.
  • EFTPS: Requires advance enrollment but lets you schedule payments ahead of time, which suits anyone who wants to “set and forget” all four quarters at once.
  • Debit or credit card: Processed through third-party vendors that charge a fee, sometimes worth it if a card’s rewards outweigh the cost.
  • Mailed vouchers: Print the payment voucher from Form 1040-ES and mail a check, or use the IRS2Go mobile app to pay from a phone.

Whichever method you use, save the confirmation number or canceled check image and log it in your bookkeeping the same day. That record is your only defense if the IRS ever disputes a payment.

What Happens If You Miss a Quarterly Payment?

Underpayment penalties aren’t a flat fee. The IRS calculates them using the federal short-term interest rate plus three percentage points, applied to each underpaid quarter separately, so a missed Q1 payment accrues its own charge even if you catch up by Q3.

Common triggers include confusing calendar quarters with the IRS’s actual payment periods, forgetting to include self-employment tax in the estimate, or assuming a single lump payment in April covers the whole year. All three are avoidable.

  • Use safe-harbor to lock in a penalty-proof baseline when income is unpredictable.
  • Use the annualized installment method (detailed in IRS Publication 505) if your income arrives in uneven bursts rather than steady monthly amounts.
  • File Form 2210 if you believe you qualify for a penalty exception or reduced calculation.
  • Talk to a CPA once your income mix gets complicated, multiple 1099s, rental property, and capital gains together.

The IRS does grant penalty waivers in specific circumstances, including casualty, disaster, or reasonable-cause situations, but those are the exception, not something to plan around.

Do You Also Owe State Estimated Taxes?

Most states with an income tax mirror the federal quarterly structure, meaning a payment you calculate for the IRS often needs a state-level twin. Nine states, including Texas, Florida, and Washington, charge no personal income tax, so residents there skip state estimated payments entirely, though local or franchise taxes can still apply in specific cities or business structures.

  • Search “[your state] department of revenue estimated tax payments” to find the correct portal.
  • Compare your state’s due dates against the federal schedule. Most match, but a few shift by weeks.
  • Check whether your state’s income threshold for requiring payments differs from the federal $1,000 mark.
  • Add the state payment to the same quarterly checklist you use for the federal deadline, so neither one slips.

What Tools Make Quarterly Payments Easier?

A repeatable checklist turns quarterly taxes from a scramble into a 20-minute task.

  1. Pull your year-to-date net profit and any 1099s or invoices issued so far.
  2. Decide between safe-harbor and current-year estimate for this quarter.
  3. Run the numbers through the 1099 Tax Calculator or the paycheck calculator to see how the payment affects your take-home cash.
  4. Submit payment through Direct Pay, EFTPS, or a mailed voucher, and save the confirmation.
  5. File the confirmation and your worksheet copy for next year’s tax prep.

Safe Harbor Or Precise Estimate: Which Fits Your Year?

Safe harbor suits anyone new to self-employment or riding a sudden income spike, since it locks in last year’s number and removes the guesswork. A current-year estimate pays off once your income becomes predictable enough to forecast, or when income is falling and overpaying would tie up cash you need now. When in doubt during your first year of self-employment, default to safe harbor.

FAQ

Who Should Pay Quarterly Estimated Taxes?

Anyone who expects to owe $1,000 or more in tax for the year after withholding and credits must make quarterly estimated payments. That typically includes 1099 contractors, gig workers, landlords, and investors without enough tax withheld elsewhere.

What Are the Four Due Dates for Quarterly Estimated Tax Payments?

The 2026 due dates are April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15, 2027, each covering an unequal payment period rather than a clean calendar quarter. Dates shift to the next business day when they fall on a weekend or federal holiday.

How Much Should I Pay for Quarterly Taxes?

Pay either 100% of last year’s total tax (110% if your prior-year AGI exceeded $150,000) under the safe-harbor method, or 90% of your current-year estimated tax if you calculate it directly. A tool like the 1099 Tax Calculator can run both numbers so you can compare them before choosing.

What Is the Best Way to Pay My Quarterly Estimated Taxes?

IRS Direct Pay is free and gives an instant confirmation, making it the simplest option for most individual filers. EFTPS suits anyone who wants to schedule all four payments in advance, while mailed vouchers from Form 1040-ES remain an option for those who prefer paper.
