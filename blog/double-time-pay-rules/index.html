Double Time Pay Rules: California After 12 Hours, Check Your Pay

Published October 16, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Federal law under the FLSA does not require double time. It requires only time-and-a-half after 40 hours in a workweek. California is the main exception, with daily double-time triggers set by the Department of Industrial Relations (DIR). Elsewhere, union contracts and employer policies can create double-time duties. We built Paysums so you can check the math against your own paycheck.

TL;DR:

  • Your regular rate includes commissions, nondiscretionary bonuses, and shift differentials, so omitting them can underpay every premium hour worked that week.
  • California pays double time for work beyond 12 daily hours and eight hours on the seventh consecutive workday; earlier daily overtime pays 1.5 times.
  • California employers may owe double time for unauthorized work if they knew or should have known it was performed; prior approval is not required.
  • A fixed workweek governs overtime calculations; employers cannot average hours across pay periods, and pay frequency does not change the amount owed.
  • Holiday and weekend premiums are not federally required, so workers should check collective bargaining agreements or employer policies for promised double time.

What does federal law require for overtime?

The FLSA does not require double time. It requires covered nonexempt employees to receive at least one and one-half times their regular rate for hours worked over 40 in a workweek. That is the entire federal baseline.

  • No weekend or holiday premiums. The Department of Labor states that the FLSA does not require extra pay for Saturdays, Sundays, holidays or regular days of rest. Any such premium comes from an employer agreement or a collective bargaining agreement.
  • Regular rate. Overtime is computed on the regular rate, which includes most remuneration for employment, such as commissions, nondiscretionary bonuses and shift differentials, divided by total hours worked in the workweek.
  • Fixed workweek. A workweek is a fixed, 168-hour period. Employers cannot move it around to avoid overtime, and overtime is measured within each separate workweek.
  • Voluntary double time uses the same base. The DOL’s overtime calculator guidance notes the regular rate must be used for overtime and for any voluntary double time.

Federal law sets a floor. States, contracts and company policies can build on it, and that is where double time actually shows up.

Where is double time actually required?

Double-time obligations can come from state law, collective bargaining agreements (CBAs) and employer policies. California has statutory double-time triggers.

California. State law requires double the regular rate for all hours worked beyond 12 in a single workday and for all hours beyond eight on the seventh consecutive day of work in a single workweek.

California DIR: double the regular rate applies to hours over 12 in a workday and over eight on the seventh consecutive workday, whether or not the overtime was authorized, provided the employer knew or should have known the work was performed.

That last point matters. California applies a “suffered or permitted” standard, so an employer cannot avoid liability by saying a supervisor never approved the extra hours. The state also pays time-and-a-half after eight hours in a day or 40 in a week, and on the seventh consecutive day the first eight hours are paid at 1.5×, with hours beyond that at double time.

Other states. Double time may be provided by an agreement or employer policy. Check the rules that apply where you work.

CBAs and contracts. The DOL’s FLSA question-and-answer page points to private agreements as the usual source of premium pay. Publicly available CBAs for trades such as electricians and sheet metal workers often define work on specified legal holidays as double-time eligible. Construction and other union trades are the most common examples.

Household employers. Double-time questions also come up with live-in and part-time caregivers. This California nanny overtime guide walks household employers through those scenarios.

How do you calculate double time?

Start with the regular rate, then split the hours into tiers. Our overtime formulas and examples guide covers the general method in more depth.

  1. Add up all pay that counts toward the regular rate: base wages, commissions, nondiscretionary bonuses and shift differentials. Our shift differential guide shows what belongs in that total.
  2. Divide that total by all hours worked in the workweek to get the regular rate.
  3. Sort the hours into regular, time-and-a-half and double-time buckets under the rules that apply to you.
  4. Multiply each bucket by its rate (1×, 1.5× or 2×).
  5. Add the three results together.

Worked example at $25 an hour. Say a California employee works a 14-hour day and earns a flat $25 regular rate. The first eight hours pay $25 each, or $200. Hours nine through 12 pay 1.5 times the rate, $37.50 each, or $150. Hours 13 and 14 pay double, $50 each, or $100. The day totals $450. California’s own worker rights guide uses the same regular, overtime and double-time tiers to illustrate a long shift.

Seventh-day check: on the seventh consecutive workday, the same employee’s first eight hours pay 1.5×, and hours beyond eight pay 2×.

Fluctuating workweek. Some employers pay a fixed salary for variable hours. Under that method, the DOL has explained that overtime hours earn an additional one-half of the regular rate, provided there is a clear mutual understanding that the salary covers all hours worked. Because the salary already covers straight time, only the half-time premium is added.

What should payroll teams get right?

Payroll errors can lead to unpaid double time. Payroll must calculate overtime per workweek and not average across pay periods, and pay frequency never changes an overtime obligation.

  • Leaving nondiscretionary pay out of the regular rate. Bonuses tied to production or attendance raise the rate that every premium hour is multiplied by.
  • Mixing daily and weekly rules. California measures overtime by the day and the week. The DLSE overtime worksheet lays out time-and-a-half over eight hours in a workday or 40 in a workweek, and double time over 12 hours in a workday or over eight on the seventh consecutive workday.
  • Rounding and averaging. Rounding hours down or spreading hours across two weeks hides premium time that was actually worked.
  • Thin records. Employers need timecards, schedules and payroll registers that show hours, rates and premiums for each workweek.

Pro Tip: Configure your payroll software for California’s daily and seventh-day overtime triggers, and test it with a sample shift before the next pay run.

What if double time wasn’t paid?

Start by confirming the math, then escalate through the right agency.

  • Check your numbers. Compare your pay stub to the hours you worked, using the regular rate and tiers above.
  • Keep evidence. Save pay stubs, timecards, schedules and any messages about hours or shift changes.
  • File a complaint. California workers can go through the Division of Labor Standards Enforcement. For federal overtime, the DOL’s wage and hour resources explain the process.
  • Ask about remedies. The options depend on the applicable law and circumstances. Check with the relevant labor agency.
  • Employers should fix it fast. Audit recent payroll runs, correct the calculation, reissue pay and update the policy so the error does not repeat.

Why checking the math matters

A small error in the regular rate multiplies across every premium hour, every pay period, for every affected employee. That is how a missed bonus inclusion becomes a large back-pay bill. Both sides benefit from catching it early: employees should ask questions with their numbers in hand, and payroll teams should welcome them. FAQ

What are the new overtime rules for 2026?

The federal baseline did not change in this research: the FLSA still requires time-and-a-half after 40 hours in a workweek and does not require double time. California’s daily double-time triggers remain in place under the DIR’s overtime rules. Check your state labor agency for any local updates.

How does double time pay work?

Double time pays two times your regular rate for qualifying hours. In California, that means hours beyond 12 in a workday and hours beyond eight on the seventh consecutive workday. Elsewhere, it applies only if your contract or employer policy provides it.

What is double time pay for $25 an hour?

Double time at a $25 regular rate is $50 an hour. In a 14-hour California workday, only hours 13 and 14 pay at that rate, and the full day totals $450 as shown above.

Does my job have to pay me double time for holidays?

Generally no. The Department of Labor says the FLSA does not require extra pay for holidays or weekends. Holiday double time comes from a union contract or employer policy, so check your agreement.
