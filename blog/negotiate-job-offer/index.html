What’s Negotiable and What to Prioritize First

Not every line item in an offer carries the same weight, and not every item moves the same amount. Before you write a single word to the recruiter, sort the offer into what is flexible and what tends to stay fixed.

  • Base salary: high negotiability, almost always the first thing to raise.
  • Sign-on bonus: high negotiability, often easier for employers to approve than a base increase because it’s a one-time cost.
  • Equity: medium to high negotiability at startups, lower at large public companies with standardized grant bands.
  • Paid time off: high negotiability, frequently adjusted in days rather than dollars.
  • Start date: very negotiable, especially when you need to give notice or handle a move.
  • Remote or flexible schedule: negotiability depends on the role and team, but worth raising if it matters to you.
  • Benefits and 401(k) match: usually fixed across the company, rarely negotiated individually.

Pick one to three asks and rank them by what actually changes your life, not by what feels impressive to request. A candidate who asks for five things at once reads as unfocused; a candidate who asks for one clear priority, with a fallback, reads as prepared. Mark any item that is a genuine deal breaker separately so you know in advance where you’re willing to walk.

Read the Written Offer Before You Ask for Anything

A verbal offer is a conversation starter, not a negotiating document. Once you have the written version, work through it line by line before you respond.

  1. Confirm the base salary figure and whether it’s annual, hourly, or pro-rated for a partial year.
  2. Check the bonus structure: target percentage, whether it’s guaranteed in year one, and the metrics that trigger payout.
  3. Review equity details: number of units or percentage, vesting schedule, and cliff period.
  4. Add up benefits costs, including premiums you’d pay for health coverage, since a lower premium can offset a smaller salary.
  5. Note PTO and sick leave separately, since some employers lump them together and others don’t.
  6. Confirm the title and reporting line, since this affects your resume and your next negotiation.
  7. Check the start date and whether it’s flexible.
  8. Review relocation support, if applicable, and what it actually covers.
  9. Ask about the review cycle: when the first formal salary review happens and what it’s tied to.

Once you have every line, calculate total compensation (salary plus bonus plus the cash value of benefits and equity) and compare it against your research for the role, location, and experience level. Then translate the gross number into what you’ll actually bank. The same $75,000 salary nets out very differently depending on where you live: it comes to $58,025 take-home in Chicago but $57,843 take-home in Los Angeles under 2026 tax tables, a gap worth knowing before you decide whether a raise clears your bar. Running the offer through a take-home pay calculator before you counter shows you the real effect of any number you’re about to name.

From there, set three figures: your target (what you’d be thrilled to get), your midpoint (a realistic landing spot), and your walk-away (the floor below which you decline). Having these in writing before the conversation keeps you from improvising under pressure.

Build a Precise, Data-Backed Salary Range

A counter that cites a specific number backed by research is harder to dismiss than a vague request for “more.” Start with the sources that matter: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics for occupational wage data, industry-specific salary guides, and company-size context, since a 50-person startup and a 5,000-person enterprise pay differently for the same title. Local market data matters too, since the same role can pay very differently between metro areas.

When you read a salary survey, look past the median to the percentile range. If your experience, scarce skills, or a competing offer put you above average, aim for the 65th to 75th percentile rather than the midpoint. Department of Labor negotiation guidance recommends looking at the whole package rather than fixating on one figure, and preparing alternative asks in case the number itself won’t move.

Once you have a market range, convert it to net pay before you name a figure out loud. A market gross figure means little until you know what lands in your account after federal, state, and payroll taxes, which is why running the number through a salary calculator before the call matters: it tells you whether the ask actually solves the problem you’re negotiating for.

Pro Tip: When the role and your leverage support it, open 10 to 15% above your target rather than naming the target itself, since employers expect a counter and rarely accept the first ask at face value.

Candidates who negotiate see meaningfully higher outcomes than those who don’t. Roughly 55 to 68% of job seekers accept the initial offer without countering, while those who did negotiate saw an average compensation increase of about 12.45% in that same research. Skipping the counter is the single most expensive thing you can do at this stage.

Draft the Counteroffer: Templates and Scripts

Having the words ready before the conversation keeps nerves from derailing a reasonable ask. Whether you negotiate by email or by phone, the structure stays the same: gratitude, genuine enthusiasm, an evidence-based ask, and flexibility on timeline.

Email template structure:

  • Open with thanks for the offer and a specific detail about the role that excites you.
  • State that you’re excited to move forward and would like to discuss a few points.
  • Name your ask with the research behind it, in one or two sentences.
  • Close by noting you’re flexible and happy to find a number that works for both sides.

A sample phone script might open like this:

Pause after naming the number. Silence is normal and doing the work for you; resist the urge to fill it with justification. If the recruiter pushes back, ask what flexibility exists, and be ready with a fallback (a sign-on bonus, extra PTO, or an early review milestone tied to specific metrics) if the base number truly can’t move.

When referencing market data or a competing offer, keep it factual and general. “Based on market data for similar roles in this region” works; naming a specific competing employer does not help your case and can read as a pressure tactic. University career-center guidance across multiple schools converges on the same approach: thank the employer, request time to review, check the full compensation picture, bring market data, and ask for the agreed terms in writing once you land on a number.

Precise anchors tend to land better than round ones. Asking for $78,500 instead of $80,000 signals that you did the math rather than picked a number out of the air, and it gives the employer less room to simply round you down.

Choosing the Right Channel and Timing

Phone and email each serve a different purpose in a negotiation, and picking the wrong one for the moment can cost you.

  1. Use the phone for live back-and-forth, especially when you expect multiple rounds or want to read tone in real time.
  2. Use email when you want exact wording on record, particularly for anything involving numbers, titles, or start dates.
  3. Ask for time before you respond at all. A typical professional window to respond to an offer runs 2 to 7 business days, and most employers expect you to take at least a few days.
  4. Request an extension politely if you need more time: “I want to give this the consideration it deserves. Could I have until [specific date] to respond?” works better than an open-ended delay.
  5. Handle exploding offers carefully. If a deadline feels artificially short, ask directly why the timeline is so tight, and request 24 to 48 extra hours to review. A legitimate employer will usually grant a short extension; one that won’t budge at all is giving you useful information about how they’ll treat you later.
  6. Never negotiate before you have the offer in writing. Verbal promises change. Get the letter or email first, then respond.

What to Say When the Employer Pushes Back

Most pushback follows a small number of patterns, and each has a professional, prepared response.

  • “The salary is fixed.” Shift the ask to a sign-on bonus, extra PTO, an accelerated review date, or a development or education stipend, since these often have more flexibility than base pay.
  • “What’s your current salary or desired range?” Redirect to market data rather than your own number: “Based on my research, the market range for this role is roughly X to Y, and I’m hoping to land toward the top of that.”
  • “This is our best and final offer.” Ask for clarification on what’s truly fixed versus flexible, then counter with a non-salary alternative or set a clear timeline for your decision rather than backing down immediately.
  • “We’re worried this will slow things down.” Reassure them you’re motivated to close quickly and just want to confirm a couple of points before accepting.

Rescission fears keep a lot of candidates silent, but the research doesn’t support that fear. Negotiation rescissions are rare when candidates negotiate professionally, and when an offer is pulled during a reasonable negotiation, it often points to deeper problems with the employer rather than a normal consequence of asking. Staying polite, specific, and flexible on secondary items is what keeps a negotiation from tipping into a dealbreaker for either side.

Closing the Deal: Confirm, Accept, or Decline

Once you reach an agreement, the goal shifts from negotiating to locking the terms down in writing.

  1. Ask for a revised written offer that spells out the updated salary, bonus, start date, and any other changed terms, and review it line by line against what you discussed.
  2. If accepting, send a short confirmation email restating the agreed terms: “Thank you, I’m happy to confirm my acceptance at [salary], with a start date of [date], as outlined in the revised offer.”
  3. If declining, keep it brief and warm: thank them for their time, note that you’ve decided to pursue another opportunity, and leave the door open for future contact.
  4. Before you resign from your current role, confirm the start date, the final compensation figure, your benefits enrollment window, and who you’ll report to, so nothing shifts after you’ve already given notice.

Following Up Without Losing the Relationship

The negotiation doesn’t end the moment you agree on a number. A short thank-you note after the call or email exchange, even when the outcome wasn’t everything you asked for, keeps the relationship warm heading into your first day. Something simple works: thank the recruiter or hiring manager for their flexibility, confirm you’re looking forward to starting, and note that you appreciate how the conversation was handled.

If part of your ask was deferred rather than denied, for example an early review tied to specific metrics, put a reminder on your own calendar and raise it again closer to that date rather than assuming someone else will track it. Hiring managers and recruiters remember candidates who negotiated respectfully, and that impression can matter later if you ever cross paths again, whether as a colleague, a reference, or a future hiring manager yourself. A negotiation handled well, win or lose on any single point, rarely costs you goodwill.

A Practical Note on Giving Yourself Permission to Ask

Negotiating a job offer is a normal, expected step, not an act of greed or risk. Most employers build room into an initial offer anticipating a counter, and staying quiet simply leaves that room unused. A data-backed ask, delivered politely, reads as competence rather than confrontation.

FAQ

Is it a good idea to negotiate a job offer?

Yes. Candidates who negotiated saw an average compensation increase of about 12.45% in one working-paper sample, while most job seekers accept the first offer without asking for more.

How do you politely negotiate a job offer?

Thank the employer for the offer, express genuine enthusiasm for the role, then name one evidence-based ask backed by market research rather than a vague request. Stay flexible on timeline and be ready with a fallback option if your top priority can’t move.

Can you lose a job offer for negotiating salary?

It’s uncommon. Rescissions during negotiation are rare when candidates stay professional, and when an offer is pulled, it typically signals a deeper issue with the employer rather than a normal reaction to a polite counter.

What is the number one rule of salary negotiation?

Never negotiate before you have the offer in writing, and always base your ask on researched market data rather than guesswork. Department of Labor guidance also stresses considering the whole compensation package, not just the base salary figure.

How long do you typically have to respond to a job offer?

The common professional window is 2 to 7 business days. If you need more time, ask for a specific extended date rather than leaving your response open-ended.
