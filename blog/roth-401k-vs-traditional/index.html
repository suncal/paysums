Roth 401(k) vs. Traditional: Test the Paycheck Impact

Published October 12, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Choose Roth when you expect to pay equal or higher tax rates in retirement; choose traditional when you expect lower rates, then test the math against your own paycheck. The factors that actually move the answer are your current marginal bracket, what you expect your retirement income sources to look like, and how your employer match gets deposited. Both the IRS and paycheck calculators can help confirm which path fits your numbers before you change your deferral election.

TL;DR:

  • Estimate retirement taxable income from Social Security, pensions, and withdrawals; filing status, state taxes, and Medicare premiums can also affect your future tax rate.
  • If your current and expected retirement rates are close, split contributions between Roth and traditional accounts, then compare the paycheck impact using the same salary and filing status.
  • The 2026 deferral cap is $24,500 across Roth and traditional contributions; workers 50 and older may add $8,000, or $11,250 at ages 60 through 63.
  • Most employer matching dollars remain pretax even when your contributions are Roth, leaving you with taxable and Roth balances unless your plan offers Roth matching.
  • Qualified Roth withdrawals require five taxable years and age 59½, while rolling a Roth 401(k) into a Roth IRA avoids lifetime required distributions.

Five concrete differences you should know

A Roth 401(k) and a traditional 401(k) share the same contribution limit and the same investment menu in most plans, but they diverge in five ways that matter for your paycheck and your retirement income.

  • Contribution timing: traditional contributions reduce your taxable wages this year; Roth contributions come out of pay that has already been taxed.
  • Withdrawal taxation: traditional withdrawals count as ordinary income in retirement; qualified Roth withdrawals, including earnings, are tax-free under the IRS Roth comparison chart.
  • Qualified distribution rules: Roth withdrawals need both a five-taxable-year holding period and age 59½, with exceptions for death or disability.
  • Required minimum distributions: designated Roth 401(k) balances can trigger RMDs unless rolled into a Roth IRA, which has no lifetime RMD for the original owner.
  • Employer match: most plans deposit matching dollars pre-tax, regardless of whether your own contributions are Roth or traditional.

These differences compound over decades, so the choice is rarely about which account is better in the abstract. It is about which tax treatment fits the income you expect to report, now and later.

How to use your current vs expected retirement marginal tax rate to choose

Your marginal tax rate, the rate charged on your next dollar of income, matters far more here than your average tax rate. A traditional contribution saves tax at your marginal rate today; a Roth contribution avoids tax at whatever your marginal rate turns out to be when you withdraw.

Several things shift that future rate: the mix of Social Security, pensions, and investment income you expect to report, whether your filing status changes, whether you relocate to a state with different income tax treatment, and how much of your Social Security benefit becomes taxable once other income is added to it. Rising Medicare premiums tied to income (IRMAA) can also push your effective rate up in retirement even when your bracket looks stable.

A practical three-step process:

  1. Estimate your current marginal federal and state rate using your 2026 tax bracket and filing status.
  2. Estimate a reasonable range for your retirement marginal rate, based on expected Social Security, pension, and withdrawal income.
  3. Compare the tax saved today against the tax you would otherwise owe on that same dollar withdrawn later.

Pro Tip: When your estimated current and retirement rates are within a few points of each other, split your contributions between Roth and traditional instead of betting everything on one guess.

2026 contribution and total-addition limits that matter for planning

Contribution limits set the ceiling on how much tax treatment you can actually choose each year, and Roth and traditional deferrals share one combined limit rather than separate ones.

  • The elective deferral limit for 401(k), 403(b), and governmental 457 plans for 2026 is $24,500, up from the prior year.
  • Standard catch-up contributions for workers aged 50 and older add an additional $8,000 on top of the base limit.
  • Workers ages 60 through 63 qualify for a higher catch-up contribution limit of $11,250 under SECURE 2.0 provisions, as described by the IRS.
  • The total annual additions limit, covering both employee and employer contributions combined, increases with age-based catch-up amounts.

The combined elective deferral limit is $24,500 for 2026, with total annual additions limits typically around $72,000 before adding age-based catch-ups. This limit applies regardless of contribution type — Roth, traditional, or a combination — making the choice one of tax treatment rather than savings capacity.

Why employer matching usually means you end up with mixed tax buckets

Most employer matches land in a pre-tax account even when your own paycheck contributions go into the Roth side of the plan. That split happens automatically in most plans and is not something you elect.

  • Your Roth deferrals grow toward tax-free qualified withdrawals, while the matching dollars grow toward ordinary income tax later.
  • This mix gives you two pools to draw from in retirement, one taxable and one tax-free, which supports more flexible withdrawal sequencing.
  • SECURE 2.0 lets some employers designate certain matching or nonelective contributions as Roth, but that option is not universal across plans.

Because the match rarely follows your own election, the employee side of the decision should rest on your marginal-rate comparison, not on how the match gets taxed.

Qualified distribution rules, penalties, and required minimum distributions

Roth 401(k) withdrawals only avoid tax entirely when they meet both conditions the IRS sets: the account must be held at least five taxable years, counted from January 1 of the first contribution year, and you must be at least 59½, or qualify through death or disability, according to the IRS Roth comparison chart.

  • A withdrawal that fails either condition is nonqualified, and the earnings portion can be taxed as ordinary income plus a 10% penalty.
  • Required minimum distributions apply to designated Roth 401(k) balances during the owner’s lifetime unless those funds are rolled into a Roth IRA, which carries no lifetime RMD for the original owner.
  • Traditional 401(k) balances are subject to RMDs on the full balance, since none of it has been taxed yet.
  • Rolling either account type into an IRA at separation from employment preserves the tax character and can simplify RMD planning later.

For estate purposes, a Roth IRA inherited by a beneficiary still carries favorable tax treatment on qualified distributions, while an inherited traditional account passes along the deferred tax bill to whoever receives it.

Two short numeric scenarios and how to test your own numbers

Scenario 1, early-career saver. Say someone earning wages that put them in a lower current bracket contributes $200 a month to a Roth 401(k). That $200 has already been taxed at their current, lower marginal rate. Decades of tax-free growth mean every dollar of earnings comes out untouched in retirement, which tends to favor Roth when today’s rate is the lowest it will ever be.

Scenario 2, peak-earnings saver. Say someone near their highest-earning years contributes the same $200 a month to a traditional 401(k) instead. That contribution lowers taxable wages right now, at a marginal rate likely higher than what they will report once retirement income drops. The immediate tax savings today can outweigh the tax owed later if the retirement rate is genuinely lower.

  1. Enter your salary, state, and filing status into a take-home pay calculator to see your current withholding baseline.
  2. Change the contribution type from traditional to Roth, or the reverse, and compare the take-home pay difference.
  3. Repeat with an estimated retirement income scenario to see which account leaves more spendable money after tax.

Pro Tip: Run the comparison twice, once assuming your retirement income stays flat and once assuming it rises with Social Security and other income layered in, so you see both ends of the range.

FAQ

When can you take out your Roth 401(k)?

You can take a qualified, tax-free withdrawal once the account has been held at least five taxable years and you reach age 59½, or you qualify through death or disability, under IRS rules. Withdrawals that miss either condition are nonqualified and may owe tax plus a 10% penalty on the earnings portion.

Does Paysums offer Roth 401(k) planning?

Take-home pay calculators and paycheck tools let you model how Roth versus traditional contributions affect your current paycheck using 2026 federal and state tax data. We do not administer retirement plans directly; for actual plan setup, work with your employer or a licensed retirement plan provider.

At what salary should you not use a Roth 401(k)?

There is no single salary cutoff; the decision depends on your current marginal rate compared with your expected retirement marginal rate, not your income alone. High earners near their peak bracket often lean traditional for the immediate deduction, but a split contribution can hedge the uncertainty either way.

What is a Roth 401(k)?

A Roth 401(k) is a workplace retirement account funded with after-tax contributions, meaning qualified withdrawals of both contributions and earnings come out tax-free in retirement, according to the IRS. It shares the same combined contribution limit as a traditional 401(k) within the same employer plan.
