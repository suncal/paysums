Avoid Overtime Pay Errors: U.S. Formulas, Examples, and How to Verify

Published September 21, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Overtime pay under the federal baseline equals the regular rate of pay multiplied by 1.5, applied to every hour worked beyond 40 in a workweek. The math only works if the regular rate is correct first: it comes from total weekly compensation, subject to specific statutory exclusions, divided by hours actually worked. State rules can raise that bar further, so the federal formula is the floor, not always the final number.

TL;DR:

  • State-specific rules can raise overtime thresholds, such as California’s daily overtime after eight hours, requiring extra calculations beyond federal standards.
  • Bonuses tied to attendance or production are included in the regular rate, while discretionary bonuses are typically excluded under official guidance.
  • Calculations must incorporate all applicable pay types, including commissions and shift differentials, but exclude reimbursed expenses and certain gifts.
  • For salaried workers, the regular rate is derived by dividing weekly pay by intended hours, then multiplied by 1.5 for overtime hours.
  • Verifying overtime pay through online calculators ensures accuracy, especially when multiple pay components like bonuses and deductions are involved.

What the Federal Baseline Says About Overtime Pay

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) defines a workweek as a fixed, recurring period of 168 hours. It does not have to run Monday through Sunday. It just has to stay consistent once an employer sets it.

Within that window, covered nonexempt employees must receive at least 1.5 times their regular rate of pay for every hour worked over 40. There is no daily overtime trigger at the federal level. Working 12 hours on a Tuesday and none on Friday does not trigger overtime unless the weekly total exceeds 40.

Not every worker qualifies. Executive, administrative, and professional employees who meet salary and duties tests are typically exempt, so it is worth checking the Department of Labor’s overtime guidance before assuming a role is covered.

How Do You Calculate the Regular Rate of Pay?

The regular rate is not always the same number printed on a pay stub next to “hourly rate.” It is total weekly compensation, minus statutory exclusions, divided by total hours actually worked. For an hourly employee with no bonuses or extra pay, the regular rate usually matches the stated hourly wage.

For salaried nonexempt employees, the calculation requires converting a weekly salary into an hourly equivalent by dividing it by the hours it is intended to cover. Commissions and piece-rate earnings get folded into total weekly pay, then divided by hours worked to produce a blended hourly figure.

The regular rate generally must include:

  • Hourly wages, salary converted to a weekly figure, and shift differentials
  • Nondiscretionary bonuses tied to production, attendance, or quality
  • Commissions and piece-rate earnings for the workweek

It typically excludes discretionary bonuses, certain gifts, reimbursed expenses, and premium pay already provided for weekends or holidays under specific conditions. The detailed rules live in 29 CFR Part 778, and payroll teams handling anything beyond straight hourly pay should read the exclusions list directly rather than guessing.

Overtime Pay Formulas With Worked Examples

The core formula stays constant across pay types: overtime pay = regular rate × 1.5 × overtime hours, and total weekly pay = (regular rate × 40) + overtime pay. What changes is how you arrive at the regular rate.

  1. Hourly employee, 44 hours worked. An employee earning $20 an hour works 44 hours in one workweek. The regular rate is $20. Overtime hours equal 4. Overtime pay is $20 × 1.5 × 4 = $120. Total weekly pay: (40 × $20) + $120 = $920.

  2. Salaried nonexempt employee, 50-hour week. An employee earns a $800 weekly salary intended to cover 40 hours. The regular rate is $800 ÷ 40 = $20. Overtime pay is $20 × 1.5 × 10 = $300. Total weekly pay: $800 + $300 = $1,100. This mirrors the computation method OPM outlines for converting salary into an hourly equivalent before applying the premium.

  3. Piece-rate worker. An employee produces units paid at a piece rate and earns $760 total for 40 hours of production plus 8 overtime hours worked, for 48 hours total. The regular rate is $760 ÷ 48 = $15.83. Overtime pay is $15.83 × 0.5 × 8 = $63.32 (piece-rate overtime commonly uses a half-time premium since the piece earnings already cover straight-time pay for all hours). Total weekly pay: $760 + $63.32 = $823.32.

Mixed-rate week: if an employee works 30 hours at $18 and 15 hours at $22 in the same week, total straight-time earnings are $540 + $330 = $870, across 45 hours. The weighted regular rate is $870 ÷ 45 = $19.33. Overtime pay for the 5 hours over 40 is $19.33 × 0.5 × 5 = $48.33 (since the $870 already includes straight-time pay for all hours worked).

What Bonuses and Reimbursements Do to the Calculation

Not every dollar paid during a workweek belongs in the regular rate. The 2019 Final Rule clarified that certain wellness benefits, tuition reimbursements, and similar perks can be excluded, which gives employers more room to offer benefits without accidentally inflating overtime liability.

Call-back pay saw a meaningful update too: the old requirement that call-back payments be “infrequent and sporadic” to qualify for exclusion was eliminated, simplifying how payroll teams treat emergency callback pay.

Labeling a bonus “discretionary” does not automatically exclude it. The DOL’s own examples distinguish bonuses tied to hours, efficiency, or attendance (nondiscretionary, must be included) from true discretionary bonuses decided at the employer’s sole option near the end of a period.

Do State Overtime Rules Override the Federal Formula?

Federal law sets the floor, and states can require more. Several states apply daily overtime triggers or higher multipliers that the 40-hour weekly rule does not capture on its own. California is the best-known example, requiring daily overtime after 8 hours and double-time after 12 hours in a single day, regardless of the weekly total.

The safest approach is sequential: compute the federal overtime obligation first, then check whether the applicable state adds a daily trigger, a higher multiplier, or an alternate workweek agreement that changes the outcome. State labor department websites are the authoritative source when federal and state rules diverge.

Avoiding the Most Common Overtime Calculation Errors

Most overtime errors trace back to a handful of repeatable mistakes rather than complicated math. Omitting commissions or nondiscretionary bonuses from the regular rate is one of the most frequent, since payroll systems sometimes calculate overtime on base wage alone.

Before finalizing a paycheck, run through this checklist:

  • Confirm the workweek start and end times are fixed and consistent
  • Verify total remuneration includes all required pay types, not just base wage
  • Recheck hours actually worked, not hours scheduled
  • Apply any state-specific daily or double-time triggers on top of the federal number
  • Compare the final pay stub line items against your manual calculation

Pro Tip: Keep a simple audit trail of timesheets and payroll worksheets for every pay period. If a dispute arises, having the raw numbers on hand makes it far easier to demonstrate the calculation was correct, or catch the error before an employee has to escalate it to the DOL or a state labor agency.

Why Manual Math Still Needs a Second Check

Overtime formulas are simple in isolation, but a single missed bonus or wrong divisor changes every downstream number on the pay stub. Running the same inputs through a calculator built on current federal and state tax tables catches errors that manual math misses, especially when a paycheck involves overtime plus deductions in the same week.

Manual calculation should always come first, since it forces a clear understanding of which hours and pay types are involved. Verifying the result against a second tool, and against the official DOL and state guidance linked throughout this guide, is what actually protects both employees and employers when the numbers get audited.

FAQ

What Is the Basic Formula for Calculating Overtime Pay?

Overtime pay equals the regular rate of pay multiplied by 1.5, multiplied by the number of hours worked over 40 in a workweek. The regular rate must be calculated from total weekly compensation divided by hours actually worked, not just the base hourly wage.

How Do You Calculate Time and a Half for a Salaried Employee?

Divide the weekly salary by the number of hours it is meant to cover to get the regular rate, then multiply that rate by 1.5 for each overtime hour. A $800 weekly salary for 40 hours produces a $20 regular rate and a $30 overtime rate for hours over 40.

Do Bonuses Count Toward the Regular Rate?

Nondiscretionary bonuses tied to production, attendance, or performance must be included in the regular rate calculation. True discretionary bonuses, decided solely at the employer’s option, can typically be excluded under Final Rule guidance.

Does California Require Different Overtime Math Than Federal Law?

Yes. California requires daily overtime after 8 hours and double-time after 12 hours in a single day, on top of the federal 40-hour weekly rule, based on state-by-state overtime comparisons. Always calculate the federal number first, then check state-specific triggers.
