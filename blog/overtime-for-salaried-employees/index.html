Overtime for Salaried Employees: $684 Weekly Floor, Model Your Pay

Published October 9, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Salaried pay does not automatically exempt an employee from overtime. Exemption depends on three tests: a salary-basis requirement, a minimum salary level, and a duties test tied to actual job functions. Many salaried workers qualify for overtime, and state law can set a higher bar than federal rules, so the paycheck impact varies by location and job type.

TL;DR:

  • As of October 2026, the threshold remains $684 weekly after courts vacated 2024 increases; teachers, doctors, and lawyers may be exempt from the salary test.
  • For nonexempt salaried workers, divide total included weekly pay by actual hours, then apply at least 1.5 times that rate to hours beyond 40.
  • State rules can exceed federal protections: California requires daily overtime after eight hours, while Washington and Maine use their own exemption salary thresholds.
  • Job titles do not decide exemption: executive roles require regular supervision of at least two employees working full time, while actual daily duties control classification.
  • Shift differentials, commissions, and promised performance bonuses count toward the regular rate, while discretionary bonuses and reimbursed business expenses generally remain excluded.

How the EAP exemption works: salary-basis, salary level, and duties test

The “EAP” exemption, short for executive, administrative, and professional, requires all three tests to hold at once. Missing any one test makes an employee nonexempt and entitled to overtime pay.

The salary-basis test means the worker receives a fixed, predetermined amount each pay period that does not shrink based on hours or output. According to Fact Sheet #17G, certain deductions can break this basis and trigger nonexempt status, including:

  • Docking pay for partial-day absences unrelated to approved leave categories.
  • Reducing salary for short-term disruptions in available work.
  • Deducting pay as a disciplinary measure for minor misconduct.

The salary-level test sets a dollar floor the worker’s pay must clear. The duties test then examines what the employee actually does day to day, not their job title. A “Manager” who spends most of the week on routine, non-supervisory tasks may still be nonexempt regardless of the label on their offer letter. Outside sales and computer employee exemptions follow separate duties criteria but share the same principle: the work performed controls the classification, not the title printed on a pay stub.

Federal salary thresholds and the current (2026) regulatory baseline

As of October 2026, the operative federal standard salary level for EAP exemption is $684 per week, or $35,568 per year, according to the Department of Labor’s salary-levels guidance. The highly compensated employee threshold sits at $107,432 per year.

These figures reflect the 2019 rule rather than the higher 2024 increases, which were vacated in court. The Department of Labor is applying the 2019 operative amounts for enforcement purposes.

Key points to confirm before relying on a threshold:

  • The standard salary level applies nationwide as a federal floor, not a ceiling.
  • Puerto Rico, American Samoa, and other U.S. territories carry separate salary thresholds for certain employers.
  • Some occupations, including teachers, doctors, and lawyers, are exempt from the salary-level test entirely under the duties test alone.

Any salaried worker paid below $684 per week fails the salary-level test automatically, regardless of duties, and must receive overtime for hours worked past 40 in a week.

How to calculate overtime for salaried nonexempt employees

A salaried employee can still be nonexempt, and when that happens, overtime math starts with the regular rate: total weekly compensation divided by hours actually worked, according to the Department of Labor’s overtime guidance. Overtime pay must equal at least one and one-half times that regular rate for every hour over 40 in the workweek.

Two examples show how this plays out:

  1. A worker earns a flat $800 salary intended to cover a 40-hour week but actually works 50 hours. The regular rate is $800 ÷ 50 = $16.00 per hour. Overtime owed is 10 hours × $16.00 × 1.5 = $240, for total weekly pay of $1,040.
  2. A worker has a guaranteed $600 weekly salary plus a $100 shift differential, working 45 hours. Total compensation is $700, so the regular rate is $700 ÷ 45 = $15.56. Overtime owed is 5 hours × $15.56 × 0.5 = $38.89 (since straight time for those hours is already included in the salary), bringing total pay to roughly $738.89.

Compensation that typically counts toward the regular rate includes shift differentials, non-discretionary bonuses, and commissions. Discretionary bonuses, certain benefit contributions, and reimbursed expenses are generally excluded. State differences: when state law matters and how to check local rules

The FLSA sets a federal floor, not a ceiling. When state law is more protective, the state rule controls, according to the Department of Labor’s small entity compliance guide.

State approaches vary widely:

  • California requires daily overtime after 8 hours in a workday, not just weekly overtime after 40 hours, and applies its own higher salary threshold for exemption.
  • Washington ties its exemption test to both a state-specific salary threshold and a duties analysis that can diverge from the federal version.
  • Maine does not automatically adopt federal salary threshold increases, so its own state figure can apply independently of federal changes.

Before assuming federal numbers apply to your paycheck, check your state labor department’s site directly. Search terms like “[state] overtime exemption salary threshold” or “[state] daily overtime rules” usually surface the right page quickly.

Practical steps: verify classification, recordkeeping, and correcting pay

Employees who suspect misclassification should start by documenting the facts:

  1. Collect recent pay stubs, your written job description, and any informal notes on actual daily tasks.
  2. Track hours worked for several weeks, including start times, end times, and unpaid breaks.
  3. Contact the Wage and Hour Division (WHD) or your state labor agency to ask whether your role meets the duties and salary tests.

Employers face a parallel checklist: audit whether job duties match posted descriptions, verify that salary-basis practices avoid improper deductions, and run payroll corrections promptly when a misclassification turns up. Clean time and pay records make any review faster and more accurate.

Pro Tip: Keep job descriptions updated whenever duties shift significantly; an outdated description is one of the most common triggers for a misclassification dispute.

Overtime pay considerations for different salaried exempt categories

Executive, administrative, and professional exemptions share the same salary-basis and salary-level tests, but the duties analysis differs by category, which matters when a company reorganizes roles or renames titles.

Executive exemption generally requires managing a department or subdivision, regularly directing at least two full-time employees, and having real input into hiring or firing decisions. A worker who supervises staff only occasionally, or whose recommendations on hiring carry no actual weight, likely fails this test even with a manager title.

Administrative exemption covers office or non-manual work directly related to management or general business operations, combined with the exercise of discretion and independent judgment on significant matters. Routine clerical work, even when performed by a salaried employee, typically does not meet this bar.

Professional exemption splits into learned professionals, whose work requires advanced knowledge in a field of science or learning usually acquired through specialized education, and creative professionals, whose work requires invention, imagination, originality, or talent in a recognized artistic field. A worker with a relevant degree is not automatically exempt; the actual work must call for that advanced knowledge consistently.

Outside sales and computer employee exemptions use separate duties tests outside the EAP framework, but the underlying lesson holds across all categories: the salary-level and salary-basis tests set a floor, and the duties actually performed decide whether the exemption applies at all. A payroll audit that only checks pay rate and ignores daily tasks will miss the more common source of misclassification.

Interaction of collective bargaining agreements or contracts with overtime rules for salaried employees

A collective bargaining agreement or individual employment contract can offer overtime terms more generous than federal law, but it cannot waive the baseline protections the FLSA guarantees. When a union contract promises overtime after 8 hours in a day, for example, that term stands even though federal law only requires overtime after 40 hours in a week, because the contract is more protective than the statutory floor.

The reverse does not work. An employer and employee, or an employer and union, cannot agree to pay less than time-and-a-half for overtime-eligible hours, and a contract clause attempting to do so is generally unenforceable against the statutory requirement. A salaried worker who is nonexempt under the duties and salary tests keeps that status regardless of what an employment contract calls their position.

Collective bargaining agreements often include detailed rules on shift premiums, call-back pay, and scheduling that interact with the regular rate calculation described earlier. Any contract-based premium pay that is non-discretionary typically gets folded into the regular rate before the overtime multiplier applies, which can raise the effective overtime rate above a simple 1.5x of base salary. Reviewing the specific contract language alongside DOL guidance is the only reliable way to confirm how a given agreement affects overtime math for a particular workforce.

How bonuses, commissions, and other forms of compensation affect the regular rate used to calculate overtime

The regular rate used for overtime is not just base salary divided by hours. According to Fact Sheet #82 on the bonus rule, many forms of compensation must be folded into total weekly pay before dividing by hours worked, which changes the effective hourly rate used for the overtime multiplier.

Non-discretionary bonuses, meaning bonuses promised in advance for meeting a production, attendance, or performance target, count toward the regular rate. Commissions earned on sales also count, even when paid out on a delayed schedule rather than weekly. This matters because a worker who earns a flat salary plus a monthly sales commission has a regular rate that shifts depending on how much commission landed in a given overtime week.

Discretionary bonuses, by contrast, generally stay out of the regular rate calculation. A surprise holiday bonus with no preset formula or promised threshold typically falls into this category. Certain benefit plan contributions and reimbursed business expenses are also usually excluded.

Because these inclusions and exclusions can swing the regular rate from week to week, a worker who receives variable pay on top of salary should expect their overtime rate to shift along with it, not stay fixed at a single number calculated once and reused indefinitely.

Practical priorities for employers and employees

Classification comes down to duties and salary-basis far more often than job title, and that is the first thing to check before trusting any offer letter or org chart. Employers get the most value from a periodic audit that compares actual daily tasks to the legal duties tests, while employees get the most value from simply tracking their own hours.

Before any reclassification decision, run the numbers through a calculator to see what changes on the actual paycheck. A test that sounds procedural becomes concrete the moment it shows up in a dollar figure.

FAQ

Are all salaried employees exempt from overtime?

No. Salaried status alone does not create exemption. A worker must also meet the salary-level test and the duties test, and many salaried workers who fail either test remain entitled to overtime under the Department of Labor’s rules.

What is the current federal salary threshold for overtime exemption?

As of October 2026, the operative federal threshold is $684 per week, or $35,568 per year, with a highly compensated employee threshold of $107,432 per year. These reflect the 2019 rule since the 2024 increases were vacated.

Can an employer deduct pay from a salaried employee without losing the exemption?

Certain deductions, like docking pay for disciplinary reasons or short-term lack of work, can break the salary-basis test described in Fact Sheet #17G. Improper deductions can cause an otherwise exempt employee to become nonexempt.

Do state overtime laws ever override federal rules?

Yes. When a state law is more protective than federal law, the state rule applies, according to the Department of Labor’s compliance guide. California’s daily overtime rule and Washington’s state-specific thresholds are two examples of stricter state standards.

How do I calculate my overtime pay as a salaried worker?

Divide your total weekly compensation by the hours you actually worked to find your regular rate, then multiply any hours over 40 by 1.5 times that rate. You can model this with your own numbers using our take-home pay calculator.
