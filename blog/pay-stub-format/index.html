Pay Stub Format: Required Fields, Layout and Examples

Published October 21, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

A standard pay stub has five blocks: employer and employee identification, pay period and pay date, earnings, deductions and taxes, and net pay, with year-to-date totals beside each amount. Federal law prescribes no single layout, so the format is a convention shaped by state wage statement laws, payroll software and what lenders, landlords and agencies expect to see. This guide lays out the fields, the order, a worked example and the points where stubs most often go wrong.

TL;DR

  • A complete stub shows: employer name and address, employee name, pay period, pay date, hours and rate, gross pay, each tax and deduction, net pay, and year-to-date totals.
  • Federal law does not require employers to give employees a pay stub, but many states do, and some dictate specific fields.
  • Pre-tax 401(k) deferrals lower federal taxable wages but not Social Security or Medicare wages, so those lines can differ on the same stub.
  • Independent contractors do not receive a pay stub or W-2 from payers. They receive Form 1099-NEC and use a contractor statement instead.
  • For income verification, add the employer EIN, a clear pay period and year-to-date figures that reconcile with the current period.

What a pay stub is and why the format matters

A pay stub, also called a paycheck stub, earnings statement or wage statement, itemizes how gross pay becomes net pay for one pay period. Employers use it to show employees what was withheld. Employees use it as proof of income for rentals, loans, benefits and identity checks.

The format matters for three reasons:

  1. Accuracy for the worker. The stub is the quickest way to catch a wrong rate, missing overtime or an incorrect withholding.
  2. Acceptance by third parties. The U.S. government treats a pay stub as a document that can verify a Social Security number for a REAL ID. Lenders and property managers apply their own, stricter tests.
  3. Employer records. The stub is the employee-facing copy of payroll data that the employer must retain and that supports Forms W-2 and 941.

Automated systems also read stubs. Microsoft’s pay stub model in Document Intelligence extracts fields such as net pay, deductions and employer EIN, which is one reason consistent labeling helps a stub pass verification.

The standard pay stub layout, block by block

Most payroll providers arrange a stub top to bottom in the order below. The order is a convention, not a rule.

Block 1: Header and identification

Field What it contains
Employer name and address Legal business name and mailing address
Employer EIN Nine-digit federal Employer Identification Number, used by verifiers
Employee name and address As on the W-4
Employee ID Internal number, optional
Last four digits of SSN Never the full number; several states restrict display

Block 2: Pay period and pay date

  • Pay period start and end dates. For example, 10/05/2026 to 10/18/2026.
  • Pay date. The day funds are issued, which can differ from the period end.
  • Pay frequency. Weekly, bi-weekly, semi-monthly or monthly. Bi-weekly produces 26 stubs a year; semi-monthly produces 24.

Block 3: Earnings

Each type of pay appears on its own line with rate, hours and current and year-to-date amounts.

  • Regular pay (hours times hourly rate, or salary divided by pay periods)
  • Overtime, with the multiplier shown
  • Bonus or commission
  • Tips, paid time off, holiday and sick pay
  • Reimbursements, listed separately because they are generally not wages

The total of these lines is gross pay.

Block 4: Taxes and deductions

Taxes and deductions are normally split into groups.

  • Pre-tax deductions: traditional 401(k), health premiums, HSA contributions.
  • Statutory taxes: federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, state income tax, and any state or local items such as disability insurance or city tax.
  • Post-tax deductions: Roth 401(k), garnishments, union dues, some benefit premiums.

Block 5: Net pay and distribution

Net pay is gross pay minus all deductions and taxes. Many stubs also show how it was paid: direct deposit to an account ending in a given four digits, or a check number.

Block 6: Year-to-date totals

A year-to-date (YTD) column repeats gross pay, each tax, each deduction and net pay cumulatively since January 1. Reviewers use YTD to confirm earnings consistency, and the figures should tie to the totals that later appear on Form W-2.

A worked pay stub example

The figures below are an illustration, not a quote for any employer. Assumptions: $30.00 an hour, 80 hours in a bi-weekly period, 5% traditional 401(k) deferral, a state with no income tax, and the 20th pay period of the year. The federal income tax amount is a placeholder, since actual withholding depends on Form W-4 inputs.

Line Current Year to date
Regular pay (80 hrs at $30.00) $2,400.00 $48,000.00
Gross pay $2,400.00 $48,000.00
401(k) traditional (5%) $120.00 $2,400.00
Federal income tax (illustrative) $212.00 $4,240.00
Social Security (6.2%) $148.80 $2,976.00
Medicare (1.45%) $34.80 $696.00
Net pay $1,884.40 $37,688.00

Three details in this example draw the most questions:

  • Social Security is calculated on $2,400.00, not $2,280.00. The 401(k) deferral reduces federal income tax wages but not FICA wages, as IRS Topic 424 explains.
  • Federal taxable wages are $2,280.00. Many stubs print this as a separate “taxable wages” line, which is why it differs from gross pay.
  • YTD equals current times 20. A stub where the year-to-date does not fit the pay frequency and pay date is a common sign of an error or a fabricated document.

The rates are those in IRS Publication 15 for 2026: 6.2% Social Security on wages up to $184,500, and 1.45% Medicare with no wage cap. Bonuses paid separately are generally withheld at a flat 22% federal supplemental rate under the same publication. What the law requires on a pay stub

Two layers apply: federal recordkeeping and state wage statement laws.

Federal rules

The Fair Labor Standards Act requires covered employers to keep accurate records of hours worked and wages paid. According to the Department of Labor FAQ, it does not require an employer to hand employees a pay stub. The Department’s recordkeeping page adds that there is no required form for the records. Some programs differ: agricultural employers covered by MSPA, and H-2A and H-2B employers, must provide detailed written earnings statements.

State rules

State law fills the gap. USAGov notes that state laws often go beyond federal protections, and that remote workers should check both the state where they work and the state where the employer is based. States differ on whether a stub is mandatory, whether electronic delivery is permitted, which fields must appear and how long the employer must keep copies. For a state-by-state breakdown, see Pay Stub Laws by State.

Typical state-required fields include:

  • Gross and net wages for the period
  • Hours worked and applicable rates
  • Itemized deductions
  • Pay period dates
  • Employer name and address

Check the state labor department for the exact list. A format that satisfies one state may omit a field required by another.

Employer recordkeeping behind the stub

The stub is the visible part of a larger record. The IRS states that employers should keep employment tax records for at least four years, including gross pay, deductions and tax deposits, because they substantiate Forms W-2 and 941. A stub that omits a deduction or misstates a rate usually signals that the underlying payroll record is wrong too.

Pay stubs versus contractor documents

Independent contractors and self-employed workers are not on payroll, so no one withholds tax from their payments. Per IRS Topic 554, they owe self-employment tax of 15.3% (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare) on net earnings of $400 or more. Payers report them on Form 1099-NEC rather than issuing a W-2 or a regular stub.

A contractor who needs to show income to a landlord or lender typically uses a combination of:

  • Invoices and bank deposit records
  • Form 1099-NEC copies
  • Schedule C and tax returns
  • A contractor earnings statement that lists gross payments, period and payer

This guide to forms and taxes for freelancers shows how the paperwork looks for gig workers in one industry. The self-employment tax calculator estimates the 15.3% and the quarterly set-aside, and W-2 vs 1099 taxes compares the two arrangements.

How to create a pay stub, step by step

Employers and self-employed owners who pay themselves can build a compliant stub in six steps.

  1. Collect the inputs. Pay rate or salary, hours, pay frequency, filing status and Form W-4 details, state, and any benefit deductions.
  2. Calculate gross pay. Hours times rate, plus overtime, bonuses and commissions. Overtime is generally 1.5 times the regular rate beyond 40 hours in a workweek.
  3. Apply pre-tax deductions. Subtract 401(k), health and HSA amounts from the correct wage bases. Remember that 401(k) does not reduce Social Security or Medicare wages.
  4. Compute taxes. Federal income tax from the withholding tables, Social Security at 6.2% up to the $184,500 wage base, Medicare at 1.45%, then state and local items.
  5. Subtract post-tax deductions and arrive at net pay.
  6. Add year-to-date totals and verify that current amounts plus prior YTD equal the new YTD.

Spreadsheet templates in Excel or Google Sheets handle steps 2 to 6 if the formulas are correct. The weak points are manual tax lookups and annual rate changes. Common formatting mistakes

Mistake Why it matters
Missing pay period dates Verifiers cannot confirm the period or frequency
No EIN Many employers and agencies cannot match the stub to a business
No year-to-date column Income consistency cannot be checked
Using the full SSN Privacy risk; use the last four digits
FICA calculated after 401(k) Understates Social Security and Medicare
Rounded or placeholder amounts Totals fail to reconcile
Reimbursements mixed into gross pay Inflates reported income

One rule of thumb ties the list together: every number on the stub should be reproducible from the inputs. If a reviewer can recompute net pay from the rate, hours and withholding settings, the format is doing its job.

Is it legal to make your own pay stub?

Creating a stub for genuine wages you actually paid or earned is ordinary payroll practice. Small employers, household employers and self-employed owners who run their own payroll all do it. The legal problem is content, not the act of creating: a stub that reports income, an employer or hours that do not exist, and is submitted to a lender, landlord or agency, is a misrepresentation and can carry civil and criminal consequences. Use a generator only with real figures, and keep the underlying payroll and bank records that support them.

Perspective: why format and tax data have to agree

We see the same pattern in the calculator every week. A stub looks professional, but the numbers beneath it do not follow the withholding rules. The usual causes are a stale tax table, a 401(k) amount subtracted from the wrong wage base, or a state item left out. A neat layout cannot compensate for any of these.

Our approach is to treat the format as the last step, not the first. The calculator refreshes its tax tables weekly from IRS, SSA and state sources, so the figures that flow into a stub reflect current rates, filing status and pay frequency. Where a stub is a deliverable, we think its test is simple: someone outside the company should be able to read it, recompute it and reach the same net pay.

We also advise treating the stub as a reconciliation tool. Compare each period with the previous one and with the year-to-date column. Differences should have a cause: overtime, a bonus taxed at the supplemental rate, a benefit change.

FAQ

What should a pay stub include?

A pay stub should show employer and employee names, the pay period and pay date, hours and rate, gross pay, each tax and deduction, net pay, and year-to-date totals. Adding the employer EIN and the last four digits of the SSN makes it usable for verification. State law may add required fields.

Are employers required to give pay stubs?

Not under federal law. The FLSA requires employers to keep records of hours and wages but not to give employees a stub. Many states do require a wage statement, so check the labor department in the state where you work.

Does Microsoft Word or Excel have a pay stub template?

Yes, Word, Excel and Google Sheets all offer templates, and many payroll tools export them. A template supplies layout only. You must enter correct tax amounts yourself, and update them when rates change each year.

How do I make a pay stub for a contractor?

Contractors are not withheld on, so a regular stub is not the standard document. A contractor earnings statement lists payments received, the period and the payer, and is paired with Form 1099-NEC and tax returns. The 1099 contractor statement starts from $7.99 one-off.
