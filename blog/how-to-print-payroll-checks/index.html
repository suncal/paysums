Avoid Payroll Check Mistakes: Platform Agnostic 7 Steps for SMBs

Published September 24, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

Yes, you can print payroll checks in-house, and doing it right takes three moves: prepare your check stock, printer, and access permissions; generate the pay period’s check file or PDF and confirm it against your payroll totals; then test-print one check, verify alignment and amounts, and run the full batch before signing and distributing. Getting each step right the first time prevents wasted stock and delayed payroll.

TL;DR:

  • Use MICR check stock and a laser printer to ensure proper encoding and alignment, preventing bank rejections or payment delays.
  • Always test print a single check after setting preferences to verify payee details, amounts, and MICR line placement before batch printing.
  • Confirm your check stock matches your bank’s MICR requirements, and adjust printer offsets if alignment issues persist.
  • Reconcile printed checks and stub details with payroll totals immediately after printing to catch errors and avoid discrepancies.

What You Need Before You Start Printing Payroll Checks

Before touching the print button, gather the physical and digital pieces that make a print run go smoothly. Skipping this checklist is the most common reason payroll admins burn through a box of check stock on the first try.

  • Check stock: blank MICR stock, preprinted voucher checks, or standard checks, depending on your bank’s requirements
  • Printer: a laser printer is standard for payroll checks; inkjet often smears MICR ink lines and fails bank scanners
  • Software access: payroll admin permissions and a PDF viewer such as Adobe Acrobat for print preview
  • Signature setup: either a manual signature stamp or an authorized electronic signature configured in your payroll system
  • Security controls: locked storage for blank check stock, restricted printer access, and a log of who printed which batch

Most payroll platforms let you set default printing preferences once, including check layout and stub content, so you’re not reconfiguring settings every pay period.

Pro Tip: Keep blank check stock in a locked cabinet, not a supply drawer. Missing blank checks are a fraud risk banks and auditors flag immediately, and a simple sign-out log solves most of that exposure.

How Do You Print Payroll Checks Step by Step?

Once your supplies and access are sorted, the actual print run follows a consistent sequence regardless of which payroll system you use.

  1. Finalize payroll totals. Confirm hours, deductions, and net pay before generating anything. Reprinting after payroll is locked usually means voiding and starting over.
  2. Select manual check as the payment method, or open the historical payroll run if you’re reprinting a past paycheck or pay stub.
  3. Set your printing preferences. Choose check layout, decide whether to include a detachable pay stub, and confirm the alignment settings match your check stock.
  4. Print one test check. Check the payee name, dollar amount, date, and MICR line placement before committing to the full batch. This single step is the difference between a clean run and a stack of ruined stock.
  5. Print the remaining checks, either in bulk or by selecting individual employees.
  6. Sign each check, either manually or through an authorized electronic signature workflow, then distribute.
  7. Reconcile and file. Note any voided checks, keep a copy of every printed check and stub, and match totals against your bank register.

Skipping the test print is the single most common mistake payroll admins make, and vendor support articles consistently point to it as a preventable cause of misalignment errors that only show up after checks are already run in bulk, according to guidance from QuickBooks Support. Reprinting a historical paycheck follows the same logic in reverse: locate the pay period in your payroll history, confirm the amounts match the original record, and print a single copy before batch printing replacements for multiple employees.

Choosing the Right Check Stock and MICR Setup

Bank acceptance hinges on two things: the check stock you use and whether the routing and account numbers are properly MICR-encoded. Get either wrong, and a check can bounce back from your bank even when the payroll math is correct.

  • Blank MICR stock works with most modern payroll software, since the platform prints the routing number, account number, and check number using magnetic ink toner during the same job.
  • Preprinted voucher checks already carry your bank’s MICR line, which reduces setup but locks you into reordering stock whenever bank details change.
  • Some banks require MICR encoding on every negotiable check; if yours does, use certified MICR toner or preprinted MICR stock rather than standard black toner.
  • Confirm printer settings before running checks: paper size (usually letter, check-perforated), correct tray, “actual size” or “100%” scaling, and print quality set to normal or high, never draft.

Alignment sheets solve most layout headaches. Print the vendor-supplied alignment sheet, compare it against your check stock, then enter the offset letters or numbers into your payroll system’s alignment settings before your test print, a workflow QuickBooks documents in detail.

Pro Tip: If your printer keeps shifting the same direction by the same amount, adjust the page offset in the printer driver itself instead of repeatedly re-editing alignment settings in your payroll software.

Print Steps by Platform Type: Cloud, Desktop, and Print Services

Every payroll platform uses different menu names, but the underlying workflow barely changes. Recognizing the pattern means you can follow almost any vendor’s help documentation without confusion.

Cloud payroll dashboards typically follow this path:

  1. Sign in with admin credentials
  2. Go to payroll history or “pay runs”
  3. Select the relevant pay period
  4. Choose “Print” or download the PDF
  5. Open the PDF and send it to your printer

Desktop payroll software usually routes through a dedicated module:

  • Open the check printing or “Pre-Check Register” screen
  • Select your check style (voucher, standard, or wallet)
  • Run the alignment tool if the software offers one
  • Print

Third-party check-printing services work differently since the printing happens off-site:

  • Export your payroll file or generate a PDF from your payroll system
  • Upload and authorize the print job through the service’s portal
  • Confirm your check stock choice and delivery method, including overnight mailing if payday is close

Cloud systems commonly bundle a “Print all checks” option directly into payroll history, while desktop systems separate it into its own check-printing module, a distinction that trips up admins switching between platforms, per Square’s payroll support documentation.

Fixing Misalignment, Blank Fields, and Printer Errors

Most print failures trace back to four causes, and each has a quick fix.

  • Misalignment: print the vendor’s alignment sheet, enter the offset code into your printing preferences, and re-test with a single check rather than a full batch.
  • Scaling problems: open the PDF in Adobe Acrobat Reader and set page scaling to “None” or “Actual size” rather than “Fit to page,” which shrinks or shifts the layout, according to QuickBooks’ troubleshooting guide.
  • Driver and default printer errors: confirm the correct printer is set as default, update the driver if the OS flagged an issue, and try a second printer if the problem persists.
  • Voided checks: mark the check void in your payroll system immediately, reprint only the corrected check, and reconcile the void against your payroll ledger before closing the pay period.

Payroll Recordkeeping: What to Keep and For How Long

Printing the check is only half the job. Federal rules dictate how long you keep the paper trail behind it.

  • Retain employment tax records, including copies of printed checks, W-4 forms, and tax deposit records, for at least four years after the tax becomes due or is paid, per IRS recordkeeping guidance.
  • W-2 forms can be created, downloaded, and filed electronically through SSA Business Services Online, and IRS Publication 15 outlines employer obligations around W-2 and Form 941 filing.
  • Electronic W-4 submissions must replicate the paper form’s language exactly and capture a valid e-signature to satisfy IRS requirements for electronic recordkeeping.
  • Keep both a physical copy and a digital backup of every printed check and stub, and reconcile them against bank statements monthly rather than waiting until quarter-end.

Connecting Payroll Software to Your Accounting System

Payroll and accounting rarely live in the same platform, which means every pay run either syncs automatically or gets entered twice. The second option invites errors, especially around tax liability accounts and net pay reconciliation.

Start by mapping your chart of accounts before your first sync: wages expense, payroll tax liability, and net pay clearing accounts need to match on both sides, or your general ledger will show mismatched totals after the first pay run. Most modern payroll platforms support a direct integration or export/import file (often CSV or a proprietary journal entry format) that posts wages, employer tax liabilities, and deductions straight into your accounting software.

Run a parallel test for one full pay cycle before trusting the integration completely. Print the checks as usual, then compare the payroll journal entry against what actually posted in your accounting system. Small mismatches, like a benefit deduction posting to the wrong liability account, are far easier to catch in one test cycle than after three months of compounding errors.

If you use a third-party check-printing service alongside separate accounting software, keep a copy of every export file you send. That file is your audit trail if a check amount is ever questioned, since it shows exactly what was authorized before printing.

Manual vs. Electronic Signatures on Payroll Checks

Every printed payroll check needs a valid signature before it’s negotiable, and you have two practical paths to get there.

Manual signatures remain common for smaller batches. An authorized signer, usually an owner or finance manager, signs each check by hand after printing. This works fine for a handful of employees but doesn’t scale well past a few dozen checks per run, and it requires the signer to be physically present on payday.

Electronic or digital signatures solve the scaling problem. Many payroll platforms and check-printing services support a signature image file that prints automatically onto every check during the batch run, using a signature the authorized signer has registered in advance. Banks generally accept these as valid so long as the signature was authorized through your account setup and matches the signature card on file.

Whichever method you choose, restrict who can trigger a signed print run. A digital signature file sitting unprotected on a shared drive is just as risky as leaving a signature stamp on an unlocked desk. Pair electronic signatures with the same access controls you’d use for blank check stock: limited user permissions, an activity log, and periodic review of who printed and signed each batch.

FAQ

Can I Print My Own Payroll Checks?

Yes. Employers can print payroll checks in-house using compatible check stock, a laser printer, and payroll software that supports check printing, or through a check-printing tool designed for that purpose. Bank acceptance depends on proper MICR encoding and correct alignment, not on where the check is printed.

Is There a Free Way to Print Payroll Checks Online?

Some payroll platforms include check printing as part of their existing subscription, so no separate fee applies if you already use one. Paysums’ check-printing service is priced at $4.99 per one-off print job for admins who don’t want a full payroll subscription.

Do I Need a Special Printer to Print Payroll Checks?

A standard laser printer works for most payroll checks, and it’s strongly preferred over inkjet because MICR ink lines need to scan cleanly through bank equipment. You don’t need specialized hardware beyond a reliable laser printer and MICR-compatible check stock or toner.

What Software Can I Use to Print Payroll Checks?

Cloud payroll platforms, desktop payroll software, and third-party check-printing services all support printing payroll checks, each with slightly different menu paths for the same basic workflow.
