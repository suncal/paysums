See Your 2026 After Tax Hourly Rate From Any Salary

Published September 30, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

To convert salary to hourly, divide annual salary by annual paid hours, commonly 2,080 for a full-time schedule of 40 hours a week across 52 weeks. A salary works out to an hourly rate based on that assumption. The 2,080-hour figure changes for part-time roles, unpaid leave, or any schedule that does not match 40 hours weekly, so the denominator should match the actual paid hours behind the salary.

TL;DR:

  • Unpaid leave or reduced weekly hours increase the effective hourly rate compared to the standard 2,080-hour assumption, sometimes significantly.
  • Overtime and flexible schedules can lead to hours worked that differ from scheduled hours, affecting actual earnings per hour.
  • Exempt status depends on duties and salary thresholds, not on converting salary to an hourly rate, so the math alone does not determine overtime eligibility.
  • Benefits and deductions, including taxes and insurance, can reduce the actual take-home hourly rate by about a third compared to gross calculations.
  • Using specialized tools to estimate after-tax take-home pay provides a more accurate picture of hourly earnings than gross conversion alone.

Formulas for converting annual, monthly, weekly, and biweekly pay

Salary-to-hourly conversion follows one core formula, with variations depending on the pay frequency listed on a pay stub or offer letter. The standard assumption of 2,080 hours comes from BLS wage data, which treats a 40-hour week times 52 weeks as the baseline for full-time hourly equivalents.

Core formulas:

  • Annual to hourly: annual salary ÷ hours per year (2,080 for standard full-time).
  • Monthly to hourly: (monthly salary × 12) ÷ hours per year
  • Weekly to hourly: weekly salary ÷ hours per week.
  • Biweekly to hourly: (biweekly salary ÷ 2) ÷ hours per week.

A worked example makes the pattern clear:

  1. A $5,000 monthly salary becomes $60,000 annually ($5,000 × 12).
  2. Divide by 2,080 hours: $60,000 ÷ 2,080 = $28.85 per hour.
  3. The same salary paid biweekly as $2,307.69 divides by 80 hours (2 weeks × 40 hours) for the same $28.85 rate.

These formulas assume paid hours match scheduled hours exactly. Any week with unpaid time off, reduced hours, or an irregular schedule requires adjusting the hours-per-year figure before dividing, which the next section walks through with specific numbers and shows how to recalculate denominators using a restaurant labor cost calculator.

Three worked examples: full-time, reduced weeks, and part-time pay

Numbers make salary-to-hourly conversion concrete. Three scenarios cover the situations most readers face: a standard full-time salary, a salary with unpaid leave built in, and a part-time hourly calculation.

Full-time, standard schedule: A $60,000 annual salary on a 40-hour week, 52-week schedule divides by 2,080 hours. That gives approximately $28.85 per hour, rounded from the exact $28.846.

Salaried with unpaid leave: A $42,000 salary paid over 48 working weeks (accounting for four unpaid weeks) uses 1,920 hours instead of 2,080 (48 weeks × 40 hours). Dividing $42,000 by 1,920 hours yields about $21.88 per hour, noticeably higher than the roughly $20.19 a 2,080-hour assumption would produce. This distinction matters for anyone comparing a job with built-in unpaid weeks against one with paid time off included in the standard 2,080-hour base.

Part-time example: A $30,000 salary at 25 hours per week uses 1,300 annual hours (25 × 52). Dividing $30,000 by 1,300 hours results in about $23.08 per hour. If that part-time role also carries four unpaid weeks, reducing hours to 1,200 (25 × 48), the rate increases to roughly $25.00 per hour.

Key takeaways from these examples:

  • The denominator, not the salary, is what changes the hourly result most.
  • Unpaid weeks increase the effective hourly rate because the same salary is spread across fewer paid hours.
  • Always calculate both an exact figure and a rounded, stage-friendly number for comparisons.

The default 2,080-hour assumption only applies when the schedule is genuinely 40 hours a week for all 52 weeks. Confirm paid weeks before trusting the standard conversion.

Why overtime, exempt status, and mileage change the math

A straight salary-to-hourly conversion assumes every hour is scheduled and paid the same way. Real jobs rarely work that cleanly, and a few adjustment factors can shift the comparison significantly.

Scheduled hours versus hours worked. A converted hourly rate reflects scheduled hours, not necessarily hours actually worked. A salaried employee who regularly works 45 hours but is paid for a 40-hour schedule is effectively earning less per hour worked than the converted figure suggests. When comparing offers, factor in expected regular overtime rather than relying on the base schedule alone.

Exempt status is not determined by the hourly conversion. The Department of Labor’s overtime fact sheet makes clear that converting a salary to an hourly figure does not settle whether a role is exempt from overtime pay. Exemption depends on salary-basis, salary-level, and duties tests, not on what the math produces or what a job title implies. A common misconception treats “salaried” as synonymous with “exempt,” but the DOL guidance ties exemption to the actual duties performed and a minimum salary threshold, not to pay structure alone.

Mileage and reimbursements stay separate from wages. The IRS standard mileage rates for 2026 changed midyear, with business mileage at 72.5 cents per mile from January through June and 76 cents per mile from July through December. Because these rates apply to specific dated periods, mileage reimbursements should never be folded into an hourly wage calculation. Keep them as a separate line item when comparing total compensation.

  • Include predictable, recurring overtime in any comparison, not just base scheduled hours.
  • Treat mileage and other reimbursements as separate from wages, especially given 2026’s midyear IRS rate change.
  • Confirm exempt status through duties and salary tests, not through the converted hourly number.

Pro Tip: When comparing two job offers, ask each employer directly whether the quoted salary assumes any regular overtime, since that single detail can change the real hourly rate by several dollars.

Separating gross pay from what actually lands in your bank account

The hourly figures calculated so far are gross numbers, meaning they represent pay before taxes and deductions. What actually reaches a bank account, the net or take-home amount, is almost always lower and depends on filing status, state of residence, and pre-tax deductions

Typical items that separate gross from net include federal income tax, state income tax where applicable, Social Security and Medicare (FICA) withholding, retirement contributions, and health insurance premiums. Each of these reduces the converted hourly figure by a different amount depending on income level and elections.

Benefits also affect the real value of a job beyond the hourly number. BLS employer-cost data from June 2025 shows that total compensation for civilian workers averaged $48.05 per hour, made up of $33.02 in wages and $15.03 in benefits. That means benefits accounted for roughly a third of total compensation at the national level, a share worth factoring into any comparison between a salaried offer with strong benefits and an hourly role without them.

What the converted number does not tell you

A converted hourly rate is a useful shorthand, but it flattens details that matter more than the number itself. The gap between a $28.85 hourly figure and what someone actually earns per hour worked can be significant once unpaid overtime, commute-adjacent duties, or inconsistent scheduling enter the picture, and none of that shows up in a simple division problem.

The bigger issue is that salaried exempt employees are often implicitly expected to work more than the hours the conversion assumes, without additional pay. Two salaries that convert to the same hourly rate on paper can represent very different real workloads depending on how strictly the employer holds to a 40-hour week. Job seekers who treat the converted number as a ceiling on effort, rather than a rough benchmark for comparison, tend to be caught off guard.

The fix is not a better formula. It is asking direct questions about expected hours before accepting a number at face value, which the next section covers.

FAQ

How do I convert my salary to hourly wage?

Divide your annual salary by the number of paid hours it represents, typically 2,080 for a standard 40-hour, 52-week schedule. For a schedule with unpaid weeks or different hours, replace 2,080 with your actual paid hours before dividing.

How much is a $40,000 salary hourly?

Using the standard 2,080-hour assumption ($40,000 ÷ 2,080 BLS), the result is $19.23 per hour before taxes. The after-tax figure depends on your state and filing status, which you can estimate with the Paysums take-home pay calculator.

What is $30 an hour in salary?

At $30 per hour on a standard 2,080-hour schedule (40 hours a week, 52 weeks a year), multiply hourly rate by paid hours for the annual equivalent. This assumes no unpaid weeks and a consistent 40-hour week; part-time or reduced schedules would lower the annual total.

How much is $42,000 a year per hour?

On a standard 2,080-hour schedule, $42,000 divides to $20.19 per hour. If the salary is paid over only 48 working weeks due to unpaid leave, the hours drop to 1,920, and the rate rises to $21.88 per hour.

Can I get an exact after-tax hourly rate instead of a gross estimate?

Yes. Gross-to-hourly conversions do not account for federal tax, state tax, or FICA withholding, so the actual take-home rate is lower. The Paysums salary calculator applies 2026 tax tables by state and filing status to estimate a more accurate after-tax hourly figure.
