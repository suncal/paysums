Avoid Last Month Rule Tax: 2026 HSA Limits for Individuals & Employers

Published October 1, 2026 · Reviewed against the 2026 tax tables · How we calculate

For 2026, the HSA contribution limit is $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage, with an additional $1,000 catch-up for account holders age 55 and older. These amounts apply only to eligible individuals: those enrolled solely in a qualifying high-deductible health plan and not enrolled in Medicare. Losing eligibility mid-year, especially during a testing period tied to the last-month rule, can turn an otherwise valid contribution into a taxable event.

TL;DR:

  • The 2026 HSA contribution limit for self-only coverage is $4,400, with a $1,000 catch-up if you are age 55 or older, and $8,750 for family coverage.
  • Eligibility depends on being enrolled only in a qualifying HDHP and not participating in Medicare or being claimed as a dependent; losing eligibility mid-year can trigger taxes.
  • Using the last-month rule requires maintaining HDHP eligibility through December 31, 2027, or else the full contribution made based on that rule becomes taxable if eligibility is lost early.
  • Employer contributions are tax-free only when made to eligible individuals, and tracking total contributions across multiple sources is critical to avoid excess contributions and penalties.
  • Confirming HDHP status, documenting employer allocations, and using pay calculators can prevent errors and optimize HSA contributions without risking taxes or penalties.

Limits at a glance: the 2026 HSA and HDHP numbers

The 2026 figures below come directly from IRS Publication 969 and apply to the calendar year regardless of when coverage started.

A few points worth keeping close at hand:

  • The HDHP minimum deductible determines whether a plan even qualifies as an HDHP for 2026.
  • The out-of-pocket maximum caps what the plan can require you to pay before it covers 100% of costs.
  • Contributions for the 2026 tax year can be made until the tax-filing deadline, typically April 15 of the following year.

Who is eligible: HDHP definition, Medicare, and dependency rules

An eligible individual, under Publication 969, is someone covered only by an HDHP, with limited exceptions for preventive care, and not enrolled in Medicare. Coverage under a spouse’s non-HDHP plan, a general-purpose health FSA, or certain other insurance can disqualify you even if your primary plan is an HDHP.

The 2026 deductible and out-of-pocket thresholds set the floor and ceiling for what qualifies as an HDHP that year; minimum deductible amounts apply for self-only and family coverage.

Two traps catch people often:

  • Retroactive Medicare enrollment can disqualify certain months from HSA eligibility, potentially affecting contributions for those periods.
  • Being claimed as a tax dependent on someone else’s return makes you ineligible to contribute, even if you are otherwise covered by a qualifying HDHP.

Catch-up contributions and account ownership rules (age 55+)

Account holders age 55 or older during 2026 are eligible to contribute an additional amount above the base limit. This catch-up amount applies per account and is separate from family limits.

Married couples need to read this carefully:

  • Each spouse age 55 or older must make their own catch-up contribution to their own HSA, not to a shared or joint account.
  • Family coverage raises the base limit to $8,750, but that combined amount can be split between two HSAs however the spouses choose.
  • A spouse’s catch-up contribution cannot be deposited into the other spouse’s account, even under family coverage.

Pro Tip: Before assuming a spouse’s contribution will land where you expect, confirm with the HSA trustee which account is receiving each dollar, since catch-up amounts are account-specific by law.

Prorating, the last-month rule, and the 12-month testing period

When HDHP coverage starts or ends partway through 2026, the contribution limit is typically prorated by the number of months you were eligible, as described in the Instructions for Form 8889.

The last-month rule offers an alternative: if you are HSA-eligible on December 1, 2026, you can contribute the full annual limit as though you had been eligible all year. Using this rule, however, locks you into a 12-month testing period running through December 31, 2027.

A worked example shows the risk:

  1. Someone becomes HDHP-eligible on November 1, 2026, and uses the last-month rule to contribute the full $4,400 self-only limit for the year instead of the smaller prorated amount.
  2. If that person drops HDHP coverage in June 2027, before the testing period ends, the extra amount contributed under the last-month rule becomes taxable income for 2027.
  3. On top of the income tax owed, a 10% additional tax applies to that amount, per Form 8889 instructions, unless the loss of eligibility was due to death or disability.

One in six categories of contribution errors traced to testing-period failures stem from exactly this scenario: relying on the last-month rule without confirming coverage would hold through the following December, according to the Form 8889 instructions.

Employer contributions, tax treatment, and reporting

Employer contributions to an employee’s HSA are generally excluded from gross income and not subject to Social Security or Medicare tax, as outlined in Publication 15-B. This exclusion applies only when the employee is an eligible individual at the time of contribution.

For benefits teams and payroll staff, a few reporting details matter:

  • Employer HSA contributions appear on Form W-2 in Box 12 with Code W, combined with any employee pretax payroll contributions.
  • Employers can allocate contributions to the prior tax year, commonly by the April filing deadline, but must notify both the trustee and the employee of that allocation.
  • Every dollar, whether from the employee’s paycheck, the employer, or a cafeteria plan, counts toward the same annual limit, so payroll systems need to track all three sources together.

Excess contributions: detection, correction, and penalties

Excess contributions happen more often than people expect, usually from one of three causes: contributing to more than one HSA without tracking the combined total, retroactive Medicare enrollment that eliminates eligibility for months already funded, or an employer allocation error that pushes the total above the 2026 limit.

The fix, outlined in Form 5329 instructions and Publication 969, depends on timing:

  • Timely withdrawal of excess contributions and earnings before the tax-filing deadline usually prevents the excise tax on excess contributions.
  • The withdrawn earnings still count as taxable income for the year the excess contribution was made.
  • Missing the deadline triggers a 6% excise tax on the excess amount for each year it remains in the account, reported on Form 5329.

Pro Tip: Check your combined contribution total every quarter rather than waiting until tax season, since multiple HSAs and employer timing changes are the easiest way to lose track of where you stand.

FAQ

Is it smart to max out HSA every year?

Maxing out an HSA is generally considered tax-efficient because contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free, and qualified withdrawals are also tax-free, a combination described in Publication 15-B. Whether it is right for you depends on your cash flow needs and whether you can cover near-term medical costs without draining the account.

Can you contribute to HSA as a high income earner?

Yes, HSA eligibility depends on HDHP enrollment and Medicare status, not income level, so high earners can contribute up to the same 2026 limits as anyone else. There is no income phase-out for HSA contributions the way there is for some retirement accounts.

What does Dave Ramsey say about HSA accounts?

Specific commentary attributed to any one financial personality on HSA strategy is not something this article can verify against an IRS source. The governing rules on contribution limits, eligibility, and tax treatment are set out in Publication 969 and the Form 8889 instructions, which are the authoritative references for decision-making.

What is the HSA 12 month rule?

The 12-month rule, more precisely the testing period tied to the last-month rule, requires that if you contribute the full annual limit based on being eligible on December 1, you must remain HDHP-eligible through December 31 of the following year. Losing eligibility during that window, except for death or disability, makes the extra contributed amount taxable and subject to a 10% additional tax under Form 8889 instructions.
