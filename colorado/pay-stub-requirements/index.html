Colorado pay stub requirements (2026)

Required by state law? Yes — and new sick-leave records from February 2026

Colorado requires an itemized statement every payday. From 1 February 2026 it also requires employers to keep a running record of paid sick leave accrued, used and available — but, contrary to most of what has been written about the change, it does not require that balance to appear on the pay stub. The stub is one permitted way to disclose it, not a mandate.

What must be on a Colorado pay statement

C.R.S. § 8-4-103(4) requires an itemized pay statement at each regular payday showing gross wages earned, all withholdings and deductions, net wages earned, the inclusive dates of the pay period, the employee's name and either the last four digits of their social security number or an employee identification number, and the employer's name and address. It is a conventional list — close to California's without the hours-and-rates detail.

The February 2026 change, stated accurately

The Division of Labor Standards and Statistics amended its Wage Protection Rules with effect from 1 February 2026 to require employers to keep contemporaneous records of paid sick leave under the Healthy Families and Workplaces Act: hours worked, sick leave accrued, and sick leave used, retained for at least two years and made available to the employee on request.

A great deal of the commentary on this change says Colorado now requires the sick-leave balance to be printed on the pay stub. It does not. The rules let an employer choose any reasonable system for communicating the balance — a separate statement, a line on the pay stub, or electronic access to the information. Putting “accrued this period / used year to date / available now” on the stub is the tidiest way to satisfy it, and it is why payroll vendors have added those lines, but it is a choice rather than a requirement. What is not optional is having the record at all.

What a Colorado pay stub actually shows

Colorado taxes income at a flat 4.4%, and uniquely among the flat-rate states it starts from the federal taxable income figure — the federal standard deduction carries straight through, so there is no separate state standard deduction to apply. That makes a Colorado stub unusually easy to check: state tax is 4.4% of roughly the same base the federal line is computed on.

The other state line is Colorado FAMLI, the paid family and medical leave premium. It changed on 1 January 2026: the total premium fell from 0.90% to 0.88% of wages, split evenly, so the most an employer may deduct from an employee went from 0.45% to 0.44%, capped at the Social Security wage base of $184,500. On a $75,000 salary that is $330.00 a year rather than $337.50. We had the old rate in our tables until 7 October 2026 and corrected it — the fix is recorded, with its source, in the tax table change log. The Colorado paycheck calculator uses the corrected figure.

Questions about Colorado pay stubs

Does Colorado require sick leave balances on the pay stub from 2026?

No — and this is widely reported wrongly. From 1 February 2026 employers must keep contemporaneous records of sick leave accrued and used and make them available to the employee, but the rules permit any reasonable system for communicating the balance: a separate statement, a line on the stub, or electronic access. The record is mandatory; the stub is one option for showing it.

How long must a Colorado employer keep sick leave records?

At least two years, and they must be made available to the employee on request. That sits alongside the general federal duty to keep payroll records under 29 CFR § 516.2.

What is the Colorado FAMLI deduction on my pay stub?

The employee share of the state's paid family and medical leave premium. For 2026 it is 0.44% of wages up to $184,500 — down from 0.45%, because the total premium fell from 0.90% to 0.88% and is split evenly between employer and employee. On $75,000 that is $330.00 for the year.

Why is there no separate Colorado standard deduction on my stub?

Because Colorado starts from federal taxable income rather than recomputing its own. The federal standard deduction carries through, and the flat 4.4% rate is applied to what is left — which is why Colorado state tax is unusually simple to verify by hand.

What has to be on a Colorado pay stub?

Gross wages earned, all withholdings and deductions, net wages earned, the inclusive dates of the pay period, your name with the last four digits of your social security number or an employee ID, and the employer's name and address — under C.R.S. § 8-4-103(4), at every regular payday.
