Texas pay stub requirements (2026)

Required by state law? No — not for most employees

Texas has an earnings-statement statute, and for most people it does not apply. Labor Code §62.003 says an employer shall give each employee a written earnings statement every pay period — and §62.151, in the same chapter, removes that duty for anyone covered by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, which covers most of the private workforce. Nearly every page you will find on this question quotes the first section and not the second.

Does Texas require employers to give pay stubs?

Two sections of the Labor Code have to be read together, and the answer changes completely between them.

“At the end of each pay period, an employer shall give each employee a written earnings statement covering the pay period.”

Tex. Lab. Code § 62.003(a)

That reads like a mandate. But Chapter 62 is the Texas Minimum Wage Act, and the chapter ends with this:

“This chapter and a municipal ordinance or charter provision governing wages in private employment, other than wages under a public contract, do not apply to a person covered by the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 (29 U.S.C. Section 201 et seq.).”

Tex. Lab. Code § 62.151

The FLSA reaches employees of most businesses with at least $500,000 in annual sales, and separately reaches individual employees engaged in interstate commerce — which, read broadly as it is, takes in the great majority of private-sector work. For those employees Chapter 62 does not apply at all, and Texas imposes no state duty to hand over a pay stub. Where the employee is genuinely outside FLSA coverage, § 62.003 applies in full.

What § 62.003 requires when it does apply

The statement must show each of these, and it “may be in any form determined by the employer” — including on a check voucher:

  • the employee's name
  • the rate of pay
  • the total amount earned during the pay period
  • any deduction made from the pay, and the purpose of each
  • the net amount of pay after all deductions
  • the total hours worked, or the number of units produced if pay is by the piece

What does apply to every Texas employer

The Texas Payday Law (Labor Code Chapter 61) carries no earnings-statement duty, but it governs the three things employers are most often caught by. These apply regardless of FLSA coverage.

How often wages must be paid

“An employer shall pay wages to each employee who is exempt from the overtime pay provisions of the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 … at least once a month.” — and an employee not exempt from overtime “at least twice a month”, with each pay period consisting “as nearly as possible of an equal number of days”.

Tex. Lab. Code § 61.011(a), (b)

What may be deducted

“An employer may not withhold or divert any part of an employee's wages unless the employer: (1) is ordered to do so by a court of competent jurisdiction; (2) is authorized to do so by state or federal law; or (3) has written authorization from the employee to deduct part of the wages for a lawful purpose.”

Tex. Lab. Code § 61.018

Tax withholding and a garnishment order fall under (1) and (2). Almost everything else an employer might want to take out — a uniform, a cash shortage, a salary advance, an insurance premium — needs the employee's written authorization first, and the stub should name the deduction so it can be matched to that authorization.

Final pay

“An employer shall pay in full an employee who is discharged from employment not later than the sixth day after the date the employee is discharged”, and an employee who leaves for any other reason “not later than the next regularly scheduled payday”.

Tex. Lab. Code § 61.014(a), (b)

The federal duty that does not go away

Whatever Texas requires, the FLSA requires the employer to keep the payroll record — hours, rate, straight-time and overtime earnings, deductions, and the dates of the pay period (29 CFR § 516.2, under 29 U.S.C. § 211(c)). It does not require handing it to the employee. The Department of Labor says so in terms: “the FLSA does not require an employer to provide employees pay stubs.” So the record exists either way — the question is only whether the employee ever sees it.

What a Texas pay stub actually shows

Texas levies no personal income tax, so a Texas earnings statement carries no state income tax line at all. What is left is federal income tax withheld under the employee's W-4, Social Security at 6.2% up to the wage base, Medicare at 1.45% with the additional 0.9% above $200,000, and whatever pre-tax deductions the employer runs. That is why a Texas stub is shorter than a California or New York one, and why the net is higher at the same gross. Questions about Texas pay stubs

Is it illegal for a Texas employer not to give a pay stub?

For an employee covered by the FLSA, no — Chapter 62 does not apply to them (§ 62.151), and no other Texas statute requires the employer to furnish a statement. For an employee genuinely outside FLSA coverage, § 62.003 applies and the employer must provide one each pay period. In both cases the employer must still keep the underlying payroll record under federal law.

Can a Texas employer give pay stubs electronically?

Nothing in § 62.003 restricts the format — the statement “may be in any form determined by the employer”, and the statute expressly contemplates it appearing on a check voucher. Texas has no electronic-delivery consent rule of the kind some states impose, so email or a payroll portal is fine where the duty applies at all.

How do I get my pay stubs from a previous Texas employer?

Ask in writing. Texas gives an employee no statutory right to demand copies, but the employer is required by federal law to have kept the records, so the request is usually straightforward. If the problem is unpaid wages rather than paperwork, the Texas Payday Law claim through the Texas Workforce Commission is the route, and it has a 180-day deadline from the date the wages were due.

What can my employer legally deduct from my pay in Texas?

A court order, a deduction authorised by state or federal law, or anything you have authorised in writing for a lawful purpose — those three, and nothing else (§ 61.018). An employer cannot deduct for a cash drawer shortage, breakage or an unreturned uniform without your written authorisation, even if a handbook says otherwise.

Does Texas require the pay stub to show accrued leave or sick time?

No. Texas has no state sick-leave accrual mandate and § 62.003 does not list leave balances among the required items. Some states have started requiring a leave balance on the statement; Texas is not one of them.
